The Migration Question

Why move a perfectly working VXLAN fabric to SRv6?

VXLAN works — "because SRv6 is newer" is not an engineering justification. This section lays out the real reasons operators migrate, and the equally important reasons not to.

Header tax and scale

  • VXLAN carries an 8-byte VXLAN header plus a full UDP/IP outer — 50 bytes of overhead per packet before the inner Ethernet frame.
  • SRv6 with a uSID-only path (no SRH in the data plane) adds only a 40-byte IPv6 header; the entire forwarding instruction is encoded inside the DA.
  • VXLAN VNI is 24 bits — 16 million identifiers. SRv6 SID space is 128 bits; identifier exhaustion is not a practical concern at any fabric scale.
  • uSID compression means multiple segment instructions fit in a single 128-bit address, keeping MTU impact flat even for multi-hop TE paths.

Unifying SP + DC underlay

  • If your DC connects to a service-provider or WAN backbone that already runs SRv6, a SRv6 DC overlay lets the same IPv6/SRv6 underlay span both domains — no encap boundary at the DC edge.
  • One technology means one skill set, one set of tooling (path computation, telemetry collectors, verification scripts), and one operational model across campus, DC, and WAN.
  • End-to-end SRv6 eliminates the VXLAN-to-MPLS (or VXLAN-to-SRv6) hand-off stitching that today lives on a dedicated border or DCI node.

Service insertion and traffic engineering

  • SR Policy and Flex-Algo are first-class constructs in SRv6 — low-latency paths, disjoint paths, and color-based steering are native.
  • Bolting TE onto a VXLAN fabric requires overlay tricks (VXLAN policy maps, DSCP remarking) that are brittle and hard to verify end-to-end.
  • SRv6 service function chaining (SFC) inserts middleboxes by placing their SID in the segment list — no bump-in-the-wire VLAN gymnastics.

Vendor and silicon roadmap pressure

  • New ASIC generations (e.g., Cisco Silicon One, Broadcom Jericho3-AI, Marvell Teralynx) are increasingly SRv6-first; VXLAN feature parity on the latest pipelines is becoming an afterthought. (Verify current silicon SRv6 support against vendor data sheets.)
  • Some vendor product lines have already sunset VXLAN feature development; bug fixes continue but new capabilities (Enhanced EVPN, per-flow TE) land on SRv6 first.
  • If your refresh cycle aligns with the next ASIC generation, designing for SRv6 now avoids a second migration later.

What's NOT a reason to migrate

  • Your fabric is pure DC with no SP/WAN integration story — the "unified underlay" argument doesn't apply, and VXLAN operational simplicity may outweigh any benefit.
  • You are at moderate scale (sub-10k VNIs, no cross-DC TE requirements) and VXLAN is meeting all SLAs — the overhead delta is immaterial.
  • Your operations team has deep VXLAN expertise and no SRv6 familiarity — a forced migration under time pressure is a risk, not an improvement.
  • If any of these apply, read §21 Decision Matrix before proceeding.

1. What Stays the Same — BGP-EVPN Control Plane

The control plane is invariant. The data plane is what changes.

BGP-EVPN was designed as an encapsulation-agnostic control plane. Swapping VXLAN for SRv6 means replacing what rides inside the outer header — the BGP session topology, route types, and service semantics are untouched.

BGP-EVPN AFI/SAFI is unchanged

  • AFI 25 / SAFI 70 (l2vpn evpn) is the same on both encapsulations.
  • Route Distinguisher and Route Target semantics, format, and import/export policy logic carry over without modification.
  • Route-reflector placement (spines as RRs) and BGP session topology are identical.
  • The only BGP attribute that changes is the Tunnel Encapsulation attribute (RFC 9012) — value changes from VXLAN (8) to SRv6 L3 Service (14) or SRv6 L2 Service (15).

Route types still mean the same thing

  • Type 2 (MAC/IP Advertisement) — carries host MAC and optionally IP; used for L2 MAC learning and L3 host-route injection into VRFs. Role unchanged.
  • Type 3 (Inclusive Multicast Ethernet Tag / IMET) — signals BUM flooding membership per EVI. Role unchanged; the encap-specific attribute is what differs.
  • Type 4 (Ethernet Segment) — per-ES DF election; mechanism and timer behavior unchanged.
  • Type 5 (IP Prefix Route) — L3 VRF prefix advertisement for symmetric IRB; role unchanged.
  • Type 1 (ES per-EAD) encap-specific attribute differences are covered in §5.

MAC/IP learning is unchanged

  • Bridge-domain MAC learning still runs in the dataplane; BGP-EVPN still distributes those MAC/IP bindings to remote PEs.
  • ARP/ND suppression — local proxy reply from the PE's ARP/ND cache — works identically; suppression is decoupled from encapsulation.
  • MAC Mobility sequence numbers and sticky MAC semantics are carried in the same BGP extended community, unchanged.

EVPN config shape

  • VRF definitions, VNI-to-EVI bindings, RD/RT policy, and MAC-VRF templates remain structurally the same.
  • Anycast gateway MAC address assignments on SVIs are unchanged.
  • Only the underlay forwarding context changes: VTEP IP → SRv6 locator; VNI → service SID.

2. What Changes — Encap, Endpoint, Service ID

Three layers of change: the encapsulation header, the endpoint identity, the service identifier.

Everything that changes lives at or below the outer IP header. The BGP control plane passes the same routes; what changes is how the forwarding plane interprets and applies the reachability information those routes carry.

Encapsulation header

  • VXLAN: outer Ethernet + outer IPv4/v6 + UDP (dst 4789) + 8-byte VXLAN header containing the 24-bit VNI. Total overhead: ~50 bytes (IPv4 outer).
  • SRv6 uSID: outer Ethernet + outer IPv6 header (40 bytes). The destination address is the forwarding instruction. No UDP, no fixed tunnel header.
  • SRH (Segment Routing Header) is present only when the active segment list has more than one entry — for most single-hop EVPN data-plane flows, the SRH is absent.

Endpoint identity

  • VXLAN: endpoint is a VTEP identified by an IPv4 (or IPv6) loopback address. The BGP Next-Hop in the EVPN route is the VTEP IP.
  • SRv6: endpoint is the SRv6 node identified by its locator prefix. The BGP Next-Hop is the node's IPv6 loopback; the service SID encodes both the node and the service action.
  • uN locator (fc00:0:N::/48 in this guide) acts as the VTEP-equivalent for reachability; each leaf's per-leaf locator (fc00:0:1:N::/64) hosts service SIDs.

Service identifier

  • VXLAN: 24-bit VNI identifies the Layer-2 or VRF service instance. The VNI is carried in the VXLAN header.
  • SRv6: service SID carries both the endpoint and the action. L2 MAC-VRF unicast uses uDT2U/End.DT2U (decap + unicast MAC lookup in the bridge domain); L2 BUM uses uDT2M/End.DT2M; L3 VRF uses uDT4/End.DT4 (IPv4) and uDT6/End.DT6 (IPv6).
  • The SID is programmed per leaf per service — unlike VNI, it is not fabric-wide flat; it encodes the destination node.

Where uSID fits

  • uSID compresses the segment list into the 128-bit IPv6 destination address — typically 32-bit micro-segments packed into the lower 96 bits.
  • For most EVPN flows (single destination, no TE policy), the DA contains exactly one uSID and no SRH is pushed, keeping overhead at 40 bytes.
  • When a TE path is required (multi-hop segment list), additional uSIDs are packed into the same DA; an SRH is added only when the list overflows 128 bits.

VXLAN vs SRv6 at a Glance

VXLAN

  • Outer IPv4 or IPv6 + UDP (4789)
  • 8-byte VXLAN header; 24-bit VNI
  • VTEP = IPv4/v6 loopback
  • ~50 bytes overhead (IPv4 outer)
  • Service ID: flat 24-bit VNI (fabric-wide)
  • No native TE; UDP src hash for ECMP
  • IPv4 and IPv6 underlay supported

SRv6 (uSID)

  • Outer IPv6 only (no UDP)
  • 40-byte IPv6 header; SRH optional
  • Endpoint = SRv6 node locator + SID
  • 54 bytes total outer overhead on the no-SRH path (14 B outer Eth + 40 B IPv6); the 40 B IPv6 header carries the destination service SID
  • Service ID: 128-bit SID (node-scoped)
  • Native TE via segment list in DA or SRH
  • IPv6 underlay required

3. Reference Topology

Used in every packet walk and migration scenario. Read once, refer back.

A 2-spine / 4-leaf iBGP-EVPN fabric is the canonical target, with Spine-1 and Spine-2 acting as iBGP route-reflectors for all EVPN sessions (leaves peer upward to both spines as RR clients). This RR-on-spines pattern is the dominant DC deployment model.

Fabric nodes

  • Spine-1, Spine-2 — pure transit (no host-facing ports); iBGP RRs for the EVPN address family. No SIDs assigned to spines.
  • Leaf-1, Leaf-2, Leaf-3, Leaf-4 — EVPN PEs; all carry VTEP function (State A) or SRv6 endpoint function (State B/M).
  • All nodes share a common iBGP AS (65000). Underlay runs IS-IS or eBGP/IGP; only loopback reachability is required.

Hosts and services

  • Host-A — single-homed to Leaf-1, VLAN-100.
  • Host-B — single-homed to Leaf-3, VLAN-100. (Same L2 segment as Host-A across the fabric.)
  • Host-C — single-homed to Leaf-1, VLAN-200.
  • Host-D — dual-homed to Leaf-3 and Leaf-4 via an Ethernet Segment (ESI = 0x0001:0001:0001:0001:0001), VLAN-200. DF election applies.
  • VRF "green" — L3 VRF spanning VLAN-100 and VLAN-200 on all leaves, symmetric IRB. Both SVIs are VRF members; inter-subnet routing is local at each leaf's anycast gateway.

VLAN / VNI map

  • VLAN-100 → VNI-10100 → EVI 100
  • VLAN-200 → VNI-10200 → EVI 200
  • VRF "green" → L3 VNI-10300 (VXLAN) / uDT4 SID (SRv6)

Three topology states

  • State A — pure VXLAN: all four leaves are VTEP nodes. All EVPN routes carry the VXLAN Tunnel Encap attribute. This is the "before" baseline.
  • State B — pure SRv6 uSID: all four leaves are SRv6 endpoints. All EVPN routes carry SRv6 Service TLVs with uSID-based SIDs. This is the "after" target.
  • State M — mid-migration, dual-encap: Leaf-1 and Leaf-3 remain VXLAN; Leaf-2 and Leaf-4 have migrated to SRv6. Leaf-2 and Leaf-4 act as redundant border-leaves (gateway nodes) that stitch between the two encapsulations. Leaf-4 is dual-homed with Host-D, making it a gateway that also participates in ESI DF election.

Address conventions

  • IPv4 loopbacks: Leaf-1 = 203.0.113.1/32, Leaf-2 = 203.0.113.2/32, Leaf-3 = 203.0.113.3/32, Leaf-4 = 203.0.113.4/32.
  • IPv6 loopbacks: Leaf-1 = 2001:db8:1::1/128, Leaf-2 = 2001:db8:1::2/128, Leaf-3 = 2001:db8:1::3/128, Leaf-4 = 2001:db8:1::4/128.
  • uN locator block: fc00:0:1::/48 — allocated to this fabric.
  • Per-leaf locator: fc00:0:1:N::/64 where N = leaf number (1–4). Service SIDs are allocated within this /64.
  • Service SIDs:
    • Leaf-3 uDT2U for VNI-10100 (EVI 100): fc00:0:1:3:e000::
    • Per-leaf uDT2U for VNI-10200 (EVI 200): fc00:0:1:N:e001::
    • Per-leaf uDT4 for VRF green (IPv4): fc00:0:1:N:e100::

4. Header Math — VXLAN Stack vs SRv6 Stack

Every migration starts by understanding the bytes you're swapping.

VXLAN encap stack

  • Outer Ethernet (14 B) + outer IP (20 B IPv4 / 40 B IPv6) + UDP (8 B) + VXLAN header (8 B) + inner Ethernet frame.
  • Total overhead: 50 B with IPv4 outer; 70 B with IPv6 outer.
  • The 8-byte VXLAN header carries the 24-bit VNI in the low 3 bytes; the remaining 5 bytes are flags/reserved.
  • UDP destination port 4789 (IANA); source port is typically entropy-hashed for ECMP.

SRv6 encap stack

  • Outer Ethernet (14 B) + outer IPv6 header (40 B) + optional SRH + payload (inner Ethernet or IP packet).
  • No UDP, no fixed tunnel header — the IPv6 destination address is the forwarding instruction.
  • uSID no-SRH path (single segment, most EVPN flows): 54 B total outer overhead (14 + 40).
  • SRH is pushed only when the active segment list exceeds what fits in the 128-bit DA. SRH base = 8 B; each additional SID = 16 B.
  • SRH + 1 segment: 54 B outer + 8 B SRH base + 16 B = ~78 B. SRH + 2 segments: ~94 B.

Header-tax comparison

  • SRv6 uSID (54 B) sits between VXLAN-v4 (50 B) and VXLAN-v6 (70 B) — the difference is 4 bytes on the common IPv4-outer VXLAN path.
  • Jumbo-MTU fabric (9000 B or 9216 B) absorbs all encap cases without fragmentation or MTU reduction on host-facing SVIs.
  • The hard constraint is 1500-byte host MTU: all encap variants reduce the effective inner MTU by their overhead; ensure host MSS clamping or PMTUD is active.
  • SRH path (78 B+) costs meaningfully more; avoid SRH in the EVPN data plane unless explicit TE policy requires it.

What's the same

  • The original Ethernet frame (L2 service) or IP packet (L3 service) is carried unchanged as the inner payload — neither encap touches the inner header or payload bytes.
  • Inner VLAN tags, IP TTL, DSCP markings, and TCP flags are preserved end-to-end.

5. Route-Type Behavior Across Encaps

Same route types, different per-encap attributes.

Scope: §1 named the route types and confirmed they survive the migration. This section compares the per-encap BGP attributes for each route type — specifically where the VTEP/Next-Hop, Tunnel Encap community, and SID encoding differ between VXLAN and SRv6. See §1 for the "unchanged" summary.

Type 1 — Ethernet Auto-Discovery (per-ES and per-EVI)

  • Drives two mechanisms: per-ES A-D (mass-withdrawal on ES failure) and per-EVI A-D (aliasing — remote PEs load-balance to multi-homed leaf).
  • VXLAN encoding: ES Label field carries a label value signalling the ES (VXLAN repurposes the MPLS label field as a VNI/label placeholder); Tunnel Encap attr = VXLAN (type 8, RFC 9012).
  • SRv6 encoding: ES Label field is replaced by an ES SID — a per-ES SRv6 SID allocated on the PE; BGP Prefix-SID attr (RFC 8669) carries the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252).
  • DF-election algorithm and timers are identical — the DF-Election Extended Community is unchanged; only the SID/label encoding differs.

Type 2 — MAC/IP Advertisement

  • Primary route for host MAC learning and host-route injection into VRFs.
  • VXLAN encoding: MPLS Label1 field repurposed as the L2 VNI (24 bits, e.g., VNI-10100); MPLS Label2 (when IP+MAC) carries the L3 VNI (e.g., VNI-10300 for VRF green).
  • SRv6 encoding: Label1 → uDT2U/End.DT2U service SID for L2 unicast MAC lookup in the bridge domain; Label2 → L3 service SID (uDT4/End.DT4 for IPv4 VRF; uDT6/End.DT6 for IPv6 VRF).
  • MAC Mobility sequence number is carried in the MAC Mobility Extended Community — identical in both encaps; sticky-MAC behavior unchanged.

Type 3 — Inclusive Multicast Ethernet Tag (IMET)

  • Signals BUM flooding membership per EVI; every PE for an EVI originates one Type 3 per EVI.
  • PMSI Tunnel attribute (RFC 6514) carries the replication mode: Ingress Replication (IR) is the most common mode for both VXLAN and SRv6 fabrics.
  • VXLAN IMET: PMSI tunnel-type = Ingress Replication; tunnel identifier = VTEP IP + VNI.
  • SRv6 IMET: PMSI tunnel-type = Ingress Replication; tunnel identifier = SRv6 endpoint IP; additionally, SRv6 supports uDT2M/End.DT2M as a native-multicast BUM action in the data plane (no need for a separate replication tunnel IP).

Type 4 — Ethernet Segment Route

  • Originated by every PE attached to a given ESI; used by remote PEs to discover all members of a multi-homed ES for DF election.
  • VXLAN encoding: ES Label field carries a label value identifying the ES; Tunnel Encap attr = type 8 (VXLAN).
  • SRv6 encoding: ES Label → ES SID (same SID structure as Type 1); BGP Prefix-SID attr with SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252).
  • DF-election algorithm (highest-IP wins by default, or W-ECMP/modulo) and all associated timers run identically regardless of encap.

Type 5 — IP Prefix Route

  • L3 VRF prefix advertisement for symmetric IRB; the cleanest route type to migrate first — no MAC state, pure IP prefix + next-hop.
  • VXLAN encoding: GW IP field carries the IP next-hop for the prefix inside the VRF; MPLS label = L3 VNI (e.g., VNI-10300); GW MAC = anycast gateway MAC on the originating leaf.
  • SRv6 encoding: GW IP / GW MAC handling unchanged; MPLS label → uDT4 SID (IPv4 VRF) or uDT6 SID (IPv6 VRF); BGP Prefix-SID attr with SRv6 L3 Service TLV (RFC 9252, type 5 sub-TLV).
  • Recommended first migration target: stand up SRv6 leaf, advertise Type 5 routes with SRv6 encoding, verify L3 reachability before touching L2 (Type 2/3) routes.

How the Encap Is Signalled in BGP

VXLAN side

  • BGP Encapsulation Extended Community (RFC 9012): type value 8 = VXLAN. Carried on all EVPN NLRI originated by a VXLAN PE.
  • MPLS Label fields in the EVPN NLRI (Types 1, 2, 4, 5) are repurposed as VNI containers — the 20-bit MPLS label field carries the 24-bit VNI with a right-shift or direct mapping depending on implementation.
  • No BGP Prefix-SID attribute is present on VXLAN routes.

SRv6 side

  • BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) is added to the EVPN NLRI, carrying the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252).
  • For L3 services (Type 5, Type 2 with IP, VRF lookup): Type 5 sub-TLV — SRv6 L3 Service TLV — encodes the uDT4 or uDT6 SID and the transposition offset/length for packing the SID into the label field.
  • For L2 services (Type 2 MAC, Type 3 IMET, Type 1 A-D): Type 6 sub-TLV — SRv6 L2 Service TLV — encodes the uDT2U or uDT2M SID.
  • BGP Encapsulation Extended Community type 14 = SRv6 L3 Service; type 15 = SRv6 L2 Service (RFC 9012).

Next-hop encoding gotcha: If a receiving leaf has not negotiated SRv6 encap capability, it installs the route with a valid next-hop but programs the forwarding entry with no valid SID resolution — the route is installed in the RIB but silently unusable in the FIB. Traffic blackholes without a BGP-level error. Audit encap capability on every PE before enabling dual-stack: verify show bgp l2vpn evpn neighbor <ip> detail shows SRv6 capability negotiated, and spot-check show evpn evi detail to confirm SIDs are resolved.
6. Packet Walk 1 — Type 2 MAC/IP, Side-by-Side

Host-A → Host-B unicast (same VNI, different leaves), traced in both encaps.

A single unicast frame crossing two leaves in the same bridge domain is the most common forwarding event in a leaf-spine EVPN fabric. Walking it in parallel exposes exactly where the two encapsulations diverge — and how little the control plane cares.

Scenario

  • Host-A is attached to Leaf-1 on VLAN-100, mapped to VNI-10100.
  • Host-B is attached to Leaf-3 on the same VLAN-100 / VNI-10100 bridge domain.
  • Host-B's MAC has already been learned by Leaf-1 via a BGP-EVPN Type 2 advertisement from Leaf-3 — no ARP/ND flood required for this frame.
  • Both leaves run dual-stack underlays; Spine-1 and Spine-2 provide ECMP.

Control plane — what each leaf installs

  • Leaf-3 originates a BGP-EVPN Type 2 route for Host-B MAC, advertising its reachability in VNI-10100.
  • VXLAN path: Leaf-1 installs a MAC-VRF entry — Host-B MAC → VTEP next-hop 203.0.113.3, encap VNI 10100.
  • SRv6 path: Leaf-1 installs a MAC-VRF entry — Host-B MAC → service SID fc00:0:1:3:e000:: (Leaf-3 uDT2U for VNI-10100), outer IPv6 src 2001:db8:1::1.
  • No per-prefix SID is needed for the MAC itself; the SID encodes the entire EVI lookup on the egress PE.

What the side-by-side highlights

  • Header tax near-parity: VXLAN overhead is 50 B (IPv4 outer); SRv6 uSID is 54 B (IPv6 outer, no SRH). SRv6 is 4 B heavier than VXLAN-over-v4, but 16 B leaner than VXLAN-over-v6 (70 B) — the typical migration target already runs dual-stack underlays.
  • Egress lookup model diverges: VXLAN decap uses a two-step lookup — match UDP dport 4789, extract VNI, then look up VNI in a separate VNI→MAC-VRF binding table. Same forwarding outcome, different lookup model. Both encapsulations deliver the L2 frame to Host-B with equivalent MAC learning and no per-flow state on the spines. 7. Packet Walk 2 — Type 5 IP Prefix, Side-by-Side

L3 VPN tenant, two subnets on two leaves — the cleanest service to migrate first.

Where Walk 1 traced an L2 frame across a shared bridge domain, Walk 2 crosses a VRF boundary. The source and destination are in different subnets; each leaf performs an IRB route lookup, not a MAC lookup. This is the dominant traffic pattern for data-center east-west L3 traffic and the safest place to start a live migration.

Scenario

  • Host-A (10.10.100.x) is on Leaf-1, VLAN-100, VRF green.
  • Remote subnet 10.10.200.0/24 is on Leaf-3, VLAN-200, same VRF green.
  • No shared bridge domain — this is pure L3, symmetric IRB. Each leaf owns its local subnet and advertises it as a BGP-EVPN Type 5 IP Prefix route.
  • Both leaves run dual-stack underlays; Spine-1 and Spine-2 provide ECMP.

Control plane — what each leaf installs

  • Leaf-3 originates a BGP-EVPN Type 5 route for 10.10.200.0/24, advertising its L3 reachability in VRF green.
  • The Type 5 NLRI carries a GW IP (Leaf-3's IRB address or loopback) and a GW MAC (Leaf-3 router-MAC). These are used by the ingress PE to rewrite the inner dst MAC before encapsulation.
  • VXLAN path: Leaf-1 installs an IP route in VRF green — 10.10.200.0/24 → VTEP next-hop 203.0.113.3, encap L3-VNI 50100, inner dst MAC = Leaf-3 router-MAC.
  • SRv6 path: Leaf-1 installs an IP route in VRF green — 10.10.200.0/24 → L3 service SID fc00:0:1:3:e100:: (Leaf-3 uDT4 for VRF green). No router-MAC rewrite needed; the SID encodes the entire VRF lookup on the egress PE.
  • L3-VNI 50100 is the per-VRF L3-VNI for VRF green; it is distinct from the per-VLAN L2-VNIs (10100, 10200).

Why this is the safe first cut

  • No bridge-domain state to migrate: Type 5 routes carry no MAC, no ARP-suppression cache, no BUM state. There is nothing to synchronize or drain before flipping the encap.
  • IRB cutover is VRF-scoped: Changing the encap on a Type 5 route affects only the L3 VRF forwarding entry on the ingress PE. The broadcast domain on either leaf is untouched. Hosts see no link-state change.
  • Rollback is trivial: Re-advertise the prefix with the old encap (VXLAN), withdraw the new one (SRv6). Traffic reverts within BGP convergence time (~sub-second with BFD). No host-facing impact; no MAC flush; no ARP re-trigger.
  • Verification is clean: A single show bgp evpn route type 5 and a ping across the VRF confirm correct forwarding on both sides before and after the cut.
Start here. Type 5 (L3 prefix) is the recommended first cut in any VXLAN→SRv6 migration: no bridge-domain state, no MAC mobility events, no BUM suppression cache to keep coherent across the transition window. Rollback is a one-line BGP config change with zero host-facing impact. Migrate all L3-only VRFs first, validate, then proceed to L2/L3 combined services (Type 2).
8. Packet Walk 3 — Type 3 IMET / BUM

ARP/flood handling. Ingress replication is the common ground; SRv6 unlocks options VXLAN can't reach.

Broadcast, Unknown-unicast, and Multicast (BUM) traffic is the one class that cannot be delivered with a single unicast tunnel — it must be replicated to every remote leaf that has members in the same bridge domain. This walk covers how both encapsulations handle that problem, using a concrete ARP-request flood as the example.

Scenario

  • Host-A sends an ARP request in VLAN-100 / VNI-10100 (EVI 100). Because the ARP target is unknown, Leaf-1 must flood to all other leaves with members in VNI-10100: Leaf-2, Leaf-3, and Leaf-4.
  • Leaf-1 ingress loopback: 203.0.113.1 (v4) / 2001:db8:1::1 (v6).
  • Remote member VTEPs (VXLAN): 203.0.113.2, 203.0.113.3, 203.0.113.4.
  • Remote member uDT2M SIDs (SRv6): fc00:0:1:2:e800::, fc00:0:1:3:e800::, fc00:0:1:4:e800:: — the e800 block denotes the L2 BUM/multicast service function (distinct from e000 uDT2U unicast and e100 uDT4 L3).

Control plane — Type 3 IMET

  • Each leaf with members in an EVI advertises a BGP-EVPN Type 3 (Inclusive Multicast Ethernet Tag, IMET) route per (EVI, Ethernet Tag). The NLRI carries the originating router's loopback as the replication endpoint.
  • Every Type 3 route carries a PMSI Tunnel attribute (RFC 6514) that declares the leaf's BUM replication mode. For ingress replication, Tunnel Type = 6 (Ingress Replication) and the Tunnel Identifier carries the leaf's node IP on both encaps. On the SRv6 side the per-flood uDT2M service SID is signalled separately in the SRv6 Services TLV (RFC 9252), not in the PMSI tunnel-id — consistent with the §5 route-type table.
  • On receiving Type 3 routes from Leaf-2, Leaf-3, and Leaf-4, Leaf-1 builds a flood list for VNI-10100: one entry per remote leaf, keyed on the PMSI Tunnel Identifier. When a BUM frame arrives from Host-A, Leaf-1 iterates this list and creates one encapsulated copy per entry.
  • ARP-suppression caches (populated from Type 2 MAC/IP routes) may suppress ARP flooding for known targets, but unknown targets — including first-time ARPs and anycast gateways not yet learned — still hit the BUM path.

Ingress Replication — Both Encaps

Ingress replication is architecturally identical in VXLAN and SRv6: the ingress PE clones the frame once per remote member and sends each clone as a unicast packet. Only the outer header format differs. The source PE bears the full replication cost — one copy per remote leaf, N−1 copies for N leaves in the EVI.

The model (identical in spirit)

  • The ingress PE (Leaf-1) receives the BUM frame on the local access port.
  • It consults the per-EVI flood list, built from received Type 3 IMET PMSI Tunnel attributes.
  • For each remote member, it generates one unicast encapsulated copy. No multicast state is required in the underlay — spines forward each copy as ordinary unicast.
  • Egress leaves (Leaf-2, Leaf-3, Leaf-4) each receive their copy, decapsulate, and deliver the frame to local ports in VNI-10100.

VXLAN copy

  • Leaf-1 produces 3 copies. Each copy: outer Eth + outer IPv4 src 203.0.113.1, dst = the target leaf's VTEP (.2, .3, .4 respectively) + UDP dport 4789 + VXLAN VNI 10100 + inner ARP frame.
  • The VNI is the same in every copy; only the outer IPv4 dst differs.
  • Outer overhead per copy: 50 B (Eth 14 + IPv4 20 + UDP 8 + VXLAN 8).

SRv6 copy

  • Leaf-1 produces 3 copies. Each copy: outer Eth + outer IPv6 src 2001:db8:1::1, DA = the target leaf's uDT2M SID (fc00:0:1:2:e800::, fc00:0:1:3:e800::, fc00:0:1:4:e800:: respectively) + inner ARP frame. No SRH — single uSID encodes both locator and BUM service function.
  • The SID encodes leaf identity and service type (uDT2M) simultaneously; the egress leaf matches the SID against its local SID table, decapsulates, and floods to the bridge domain.
  • Outer overhead per copy: 54 B (Eth 14 + IPv6 40). No SRH overhead.

Beyond ingress replication, each encapsulation family offers an alternative BUM mechanism. VXLAN's alternative (PIM-based multicast underlay) is a legacy option that adds operational complexity. SRv6's alternative (in-fabric replication via uDT2M / End.DT2M at a designated replicator) is a net improvement and requires no underlay multicast state.

VXLAN-specific: PIM-based multicast underlay (legacy)

  • Instead of ingress replication, the underlay runs PIM (typically PIM-SSM or PIM-SM). Each VNI is mapped to a multicast group address; BUM traffic is delivered via the underlay tree rather than N unicast copies.
  • Advantage: ingress PE sends one copy; the multicast tree handles replication at the spines. For large EVIs (many leaves) this reduces egress bandwidth at the source PE.
  • Disadvantage: requires PIM on all spines and leaves; adds protocol complexity and per-group state in the underlay. In practice most modern DC deployments use ingress replication exclusively and avoid underlay multicast altogether.
  • Prevalence: uncommon in greenfield deployments; more likely in legacy brownfield fabrics that were built before ingress replication was widely deployed.

SRv6-specific: in-fabric replication via uDT2M / End.DT2M

  • A designated fabric replicator node (e.g., a spine or a dedicated replicator leaf) is configured with an End.DT2M (or uDT2M in compressed uSID form) behavior. The ingress PE sends a single copy to the replicator's SID; the replicator expands and forwards to all member leaves.
  • This shifts the replication fan-out cost from the source PE to a purpose-built or over-provisioned replicator, reducing ingress PE compute and bandwidth for large EVIs.
  • No PIM, no underlay multicast state — replication is controlled entirely by the SRv6 policy and the replicator's SID table. Operationally cleaner than PIM-based underlay.
  • Not required for basic migration; ingress replication with uDT2M SIDs (as walked above) is the default and covers most deployments.

Migration implication

  • Default: use ingress replication on both sides. The PMSI Tunnel Type 6 (Ingress Replication) attribute is the same in both VXLAN and SRv6 BGP advertisements; only the Tunnel Identifier value changes (VTEP address → uDT2M SID). This minimizes migration risk and keeps the BUM plane structurally identical across the transition.
  • If your VXLAN deployment uses PIM underlay: the PIM-to-ingress-replication shift is a separate workstream. Do not conflate it with the VXLAN→SRv6 encap migration. Retire the PIM underlay after validating ingress replication for all VNIs, ideally in a maintenance window before the SRv6 cutover.
  • ARP suppression state: The suppression cache (populated from Type 2 routes) carries over to the SRv6 plane unchanged, since it lives in the BGP control plane. Unknown-target ARPs still exercise the BUM path; verify the uDT2M flood list on the new plane before completing the cutover.
PIM underlay retirement: If your VXLAN deployment uses a PIM multicast underlay for BUM delivery, migrating to SRv6 is also an opportunity to drop PIM entirely — uDT2M ingress replication (or in-fabric replication) requires zero underlay multicast state. However, plan the underlay-multicast retirement as a separate workstream from the encap migration. Conflating the two changes makes fault isolation harder and extends the maintenance window. Retire PIM per-VNI after validating ingress replication on the VXLAN plane first, then proceed with the SRv6 cutover.
9. Hardware, Silicon, Scale, MTU, Operational Tooling

Where the comparison stops being protocol theory and becomes a buying decision.

Protocol correctness is necessary but not sufficient. Whether a migration is feasible in your fabric depends on four hardware-and-operations questions: does your silicon support SRv6/uSID at line rate; what are the real scale limits; where does MTU math break down; and how much re-tooling do operators face post-cutover?

Silicon support

  • VXLAN encap/decap at line rate is broadly supported — Broadcom Trident3/4, Tomahawk3/4, Jericho2/3, Cisco Silicon One, NVIDIA/Mellanox Spectrum-2/3, and Marvell Teralynx all handle VXLAN in silicon. This is a mature, widely deployed capability.
  • SRv6 uSID hardware support is newer and pipeline-dependent. Jericho2/3 (carrier-grade) and select Tomahawk4 configurations, Cisco Silicon One G3, and Spectrum-4 have published SRv6 support; Trident3 and earlier Tomahawk generations vary by capability tier. Always verify the specific SRv6/uSID feature set against current vendor data sheets — silicon roadmaps move faster than any written guide.
  • "Does my existing leaf ASIC support SRv6 uSID in hardware?" is THE gating question before any migration scope is committed. A leaf that falls back to software SRv6 decap will not sustain line-rate traffic at scale.
  • Verify: uSID compression support (not just SRv6 base); endpoint behaviors needed (End.DT2U / uDT2U, End.DT2M / uDT2M, End.DT4 / uDT4); SRH parsing depth in the pipeline (even if you deploy no-SRH paths today, ensure the pipeline won't drop SRH packets in transit).
  • Generic families for reference — verify current SRv6/uSID support against vendor data sheets: Broadcom Trident/Tomahawk/Jericho, Cisco Silicon One, NVIDIA/Mellanox Spectrum, Marvell Teralynx.

Scale

  • VXLAN VNI space: 24 bits → ~16 million theoretical identifiers. In practice, vendor implementations cap lower (often 4 K–64 K active VNIs per device depending on ASIC and memory allocation). Identifier exhaustion is rare in pure-DC deployments but relevant for multi-tenant or DCI-at-scale scenarios.
  • SRv6 SID space: 128 bits. The locator + function encoding (e.g., /48 node locator + 16-bit function block) yields millions of service SIDs per node; identifier exhaustion is not a practical concern at any realistic fabric scale.
  • Underlay routing-table growth: migrating from IPv4 VTEP loopbacks to IPv6 SRv6 locators replaces a /32-per-leaf IPv4 table with a /48-per-leaf (or /64-per-leaf) IPv6 table. Route count stays the same; prefix length and address family change. ISIS or OSPF re-convergence behavior is equivalent but verify IPv6 scale limits on your spines.
  • SRv6 introduces per-leaf locator prefixes (/48 aggregate + per-function /128s in the SID table). Size your RIB/FIB capacity for the IPv6 SID table in addition to underlay prefixes.

MTU implications

  • Overhead from §4 (exact figures): VXLAN-v4 = 50 B; VXLAN-v6 = 70 B; SRv6 uSID no-SRH = 54 B; SRv6 SRH + 1 segment ≈ 78 B.
  • Standard 1500 B host MTU + 50–78 B encap overhead requires a fabric MTU of at least 1578 B. A 9000 B jumbo fabric absorbs all variants with margin to spare — verify your fabric is already jumbo before migration begins, not during.
  • SRH paths cost more: each additional SID in an SRH adds 16 B. If your SR Policy or Flex-Algo paths push SRH, size for the worst-case segment count at the fabric design stage. A two-SID SRH path (94 B overhead) still fits inside a 9000 B fabric with a 1500 B host MTU, but verify explicitly.
  • Host-facing interfaces: ensure MSS clamping (for TCP) and PMTUD (for non-TCP) are active on all SVIs/IRBs before and after cutover. The encap technology changes; the PMTUD requirement does not.
  • Verify MTU consistency end-to-end — leaf uplinks, spine ports, DCI/WAN interfaces. A single under-sized interface silently fragments or drops packets at the worst possible moment (during a maintenance window).

Operational tooling

  • Packet capture filters change completely:
    • VXLAN: udp dst port 4789 — plaintext, VNI readable in byte offset 8 of the UDP payload. Wireshark dissects natively.
    • SRv6 (no SRH): filter on IPv6 destination address matching your locator block (e.g., ip6 dst fc00::/16). The service SID encodes EVI/VRF inline.
    • SRv6 (SRH path): add ip6 proto 43 (next-header = Routing Extension Header) to catch packets with an active segment list.
  • Telemetry counters are re-keyed: per-VNI byte/packet counters (keyed on a 24-bit VNI) become per-SID counters (keyed on a 128-bit IPv6 address or a vendor-specific SID index). Update dashboards, alert thresholds, and capacity-planning queries before cutover. Historical VNI-keyed data does not automatically map to the new SID key space.
  • Underlay troubleshooting: traceroute behavior changes — VXLAN UDP-encapped flows are traced via inner UDP entropy; SRv6 flows trace as native IPv6 to the destination SID. Tools like mpls traceroute are not relevant; standard IPv6 path tools apply.
  • Vendor-specific CLI for SRv6 SID state (show segment-routing srv6 sid or equivalent) is a new command set to add to runbooks. Most vendors ship this alongside SRv6 support; verify the CLI surface on your platform before the cutover window.

What stays the same operationally

  • show bgp l2vpn evpn — route-type output, next-hop resolution, and community display are structurally unchanged. The tunnel encap attribute value changes (VXLAN → SRv6); everything else reads the same.
  • show evpn mac / show evpn mac-ip — MAC table, MAC-IP bindings, ESI membership, and DF election state are protocol-identical. The remote-endpoint display changes from VTEP IPv4 to SID IPv6, but the table shape does not.
  • VRF route tables (show ip route vrf / show ipv6 route vrf) — overlay routes injected from EVPN remain in the same VRF; the nexthop resolves via the SRv6 SID rather than a VTEP, but the VRF forwarding model is unchanged.
  • BGP session management, peer-group configuration, route-policy/import-export logic, and community strings are all unaffected by the encap migration. EVPN address-family stays the same; only the per-route SID/VNI encoding changes.

10. Coexistence Baseline — Dual-Stack Fabric Design

Coexistence isn't transient — operators sit in it for months. Design it well.

State M is not a brief waypoint. In real fabrics it persists for weeks or months while leaf upgrades, hardware audits, and per-service cutovers proceed rack by rack. That means the dual-encap state must be operationally stable, monitorable, and understood as a steady design — not a mistake to be escaped quickly.

What dual-stack means here

  • The same iBGP-EVPN fabric simultaneously advertises VXLAN routes and SRv6 routes. Both encap families are active on the control plane at the same time.
  • Leaves are categorised into three roles: VXLAN-only (not yet migrated), SRv6-only (fully migrated), and dual-encap gateway (Leaf-2 / Leaf-4 in State M) — can originate and terminate both encaps and stitch between them.
  • Control plane carries both attribute families: BGP Encapsulation Extended Community (RFC 9012, type 8) for VXLAN routes, and BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) carrying the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252) for SRv6 routes. A leaf that understands only one family ignores the other's routes silently — this is expected and correct.
  • Traffic between a VXLAN-only leaf and an SRv6-only leaf must transit a gateway. Direct cross-encap forwarding is not possible without stitching at a dual-encap node.

Underlay requirements

  • IPv4 underlay stays intact for VXLAN: existing IPv4 loopbacks (203.0.113.x/32) and IS-IS/IGP sessions are unchanged. VTEP reachability remains on the IPv4 plane throughout State M.
  • IPv6 underlay required for SRv6: each leaf being migrated must have an IPv6 loopback reachable fabric-wide (2001:db8:1::N/128) and a per-leaf locator prefix (fc00:0:1:N::/64) redistributed into the underlay IGP.
  • uN locator block fc00:0:1::/48 must be allocated and summarised at the fabric boundary before the first leaf is migrated. Do not advertise per-leaf /64s externally — aggregate at the border.
  • IS-IS IPv6 multi-topology (or dual-stack OSPFv3) must be enabled on all spines and migrated leaves. VXLAN-only leaves do not need IPv6 IGP participation but must not filter IPv6 locator advertisements in transit.
  • MTU headroom: verify per §4 figures before migration. A 9000 B jumbo fabric accommodates all encap variants; a 1500 B fabric requires careful sizing even at State M.

Control-plane design

  • RR on Spine-1 and Spine-2 (per §3) carry both AFI/SAFI for the EVPN address family. No additional AFI/SAFI configuration is required — EVPN (AFI 25, SAFI 70) carries both VXLAN and SRv6 NLRI in the same session.
  • iBGP next-hop-unchanged at the RR: spines must reflect EVPN next-hops unchanged so that leaves resolve VTEP IPv4 addresses and SRv6 IPv6 locators against their own underlay RIBs. Do not translate or modify next-hops at the RR.
  • No separate BGP session or peer-group is required for the coexistence period. VXLAN and SRv6 routes are distinguished entirely by per-route attributes (Encap Ext Community vs. Prefix-SID), not by session topology.
  • Route reflectors should have add-path enabled for EVPN if multiple paths per prefix are expected — relevant for anycast gateways and ESI multi-homing across encap domains.

Pre-migration sanity checks

  • No duplicate VNI/SID semantics: ensure that VNI assignments (VLAN-100 → VNI-10100, VLAN-200 → VNI-10200, VRF green → L3 VNI-10300) are consistent across all leaves before introducing SRv6 SIDs. A VNI mismatch discovered mid-migration is far harder to resolve under dual-encap.
  • MTU headroom: confirm fabric MTU ≥ 1578 B (1500 B host + 78 B SRv6 SRH worst-case) end-to-end. Per §4 figures; no exceptions.
  • Per-leaf hardware SRv6 support audit: per §9, every leaf to be migrated must pass the silicon audit — uSID compression, required End.* behaviors (uDT2U, uDT2M, uDT4), and SRH parsing depth. No leaf enters State M until its ASIC is confirmed capable.
  • BGP encap capability awareness: inventory which leaves support which encap. A leaf that receives an SRv6-attributed route and lacks SRv6 capability will install the route but silently fail to forward — see the next-hop gotcha callout below.
  • Locator allocation: allocate all per-leaf locators from the fc00:0:1::/48 block up front, even for leaves that will not be migrated immediately. Avoids renumbering mid-migration.

How Each Encap Is Signalled

VXLAN side

  • BGP Encapsulation Extended Community (RFC 9012) — type value 8 = VXLAN. Carried on every EVPN NLRI originated by a VXLAN PE.
  • The MPLS Label1 field in the EVPN NLRI is repurposed to carry the VNI (24-bit value, leftmost 20 bits). This is how VXLAN VNI is encoded in a BGP route — there is no separate VNI field.
  • Next-hop = IPv4 VTEP loopback (e.g., 203.0.113.1). Forwarding is to the VTEP address; outer IP is IPv4.
  • No BGP Prefix-SID attribute is present on VXLAN routes. A peer that only understands Prefix-SID will not install these routes as SRv6.

SRv6 side

  • BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) is added to the EVPN NLRI, carrying the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252).
  • Within the SRv6 Service TLV: Type 5 sub-TLV = L3 service (used for Type 5 IP Prefix / VRF routes, carrying uDT4/uDT6 SID); Type 6 sub-TLV = L2 service (used for Type 2/3 MAC/MAC-IP routes, carrying uDT2U/uDT2M SID).
  • Next-hop = IPv6 loopback or locator address (e.g., 2001:db8:1::2). Outer IP is IPv6; no UDP encapsulation (uSID no-SRH path).
  • BGP Encapsulation Extended Community type 14 = SRv6 L3 Service or type 15 = SRv6 L2 Service may also be present (RFC 9012) to aid encap selection at peers that key on the Ext Community rather than the Prefix-SID attribute.
Next-hop encoding gotcha: if a leaf advertises a route with an SRv6 next-hop (IPv6 locator) and a peer does not understand SRv6, the route may install in the peer's RIB but will be silently unusable — the peer has no IPv6 forwarding path to the locator. This is the most common silent failure mode during coexistence. Audit encap capability on every receiving leaf before enabling dual-stack advertisement. A quick check: show bgp l2vpn evpn next-hop and verify IPv6 next-hops resolve in the peer's underlay RIB.
11. Coexistence — What It Means Per Service

Phase 1 sits quietly for weeks. Here's what to watch in each service while it does.

Each EVPN service type behaves differently under dual-encap. L3/Type 5 is the cleanest to coexist — purely a per-route attribute swap. L2/Type 2 coexistence is stable but cross-encap MAC mobility requires gateway mediation. IRB (symmetric) introduces anycast gateway consistency requirements. Multi-homing adds split-horizon and DF election complexity across the encap boundary. Understanding per-service behaviour is what separates a controlled Phase 1 from an unplanned one.

L2 (Type 2 — MAC/IP)

  • VNI semantics are preserved on both encaps: VLAN-100 maps to VNI-10100 regardless of encap. The EVI number is the same; only the tunnel attribute differs.
  • MAC learning works correctly on both encaps independently — a VXLAN leaf learns remote MACs via VXLAN-attributed Type 2 routes; an SRv6 leaf learns them via SRv6-attributed Type 2 routes. Each leaf installs the routes it understands.
  • Cross-encap MAC mobility requires the gateway. If Host-A (behind Leaf-1, VXLAN) moves to Leaf-3 (also VXLAN), standard MAC mobility applies. If a host moves to or from the SRv6 domain, the gateway (Leaf-2/Leaf-4) must re-originate the Type 2 route in the other encap with the correct sequence number. See §14 for the full MAC mobility walk.
  • Duplicate MAC detection operates per-encap domain first, then at the gateway. Ensure the gateway propagates MAC mobility sequence numbers faithfully across the encap boundary.

L3 (Type 5 — IP Prefix)

  • A pure-L3 VRF (e.g., VRF green) can be simultaneously advertised by VXLAN leaves (using BGP Encap Ext Community type 8, L3 VNI-10300) and SRv6 leaves (using BGP Prefix-SID RFC 8669 / SRv6 Service TLV RFC 9252 type 5 sub-TLV, uDT4 SID fc00:0:1:N:e100::). Both are valid Type 5 routes; they differ only in encap attribute.
  • Receiving side picks by encap support and local policy. An SRv6-capable leaf prefers the SRv6-attributed route; a VXLAN-only leaf installs the VXLAN route. No explicit policy configuration is needed if both are advertised.
  • Cleanest service to migrate first — no MAC table, no ARP state, no split-horizon complexity. A pure L3 VRF cutover is reversible (re-advertise VXLAN) and has minimal blast radius if something is mis-configured.
  • Verify VRF import/export route-targets are identical on both encaps. A mismatched RT means the SRv6 route is not imported into the target VRF even if the signalling is correct.

IRB (symmetric)

  • Anycast gateway MAC and IP must be consistent across all leaves on both encaps. The anycast gateway MAC (e.g., 00:00:5e:00:01:xx) and the SVI IP address must be identical fabric-wide. This is not a new requirement — it applies in pure VXLAN too — but a gateway node in State M that misconfigures its SVI IP will break IRB for the entire segment.
  • Routing is protocol-identical: symmetric IRB does a route-lookup at the ingress leaf (egress SID or VTEP), encapsulates to the egress leaf's L3 endpoint, and decapsulates at the egress leaf. The encap technology does not change the IRB logic.
  • Bridging semantics differ: on the VXLAN side, the EVI is identified by VNI-10100/10200; on the SRv6 side, the same EVI is identified by the uDT2U SID. The gateway must maintain consistent EVI membership on both encap planes.
  • ARP/ND suppression operates per-encap. Ensure the gateway populates its ARP/ND cache from both encap planes and re-advertises ARP entries across the encap boundary in Type 2 routes.

Multi-homing (ESI — Type 1 + Type 4)

  • The Ethernet Segment route (Type 4) is advertised by both Leaf-3 and Leaf-4 for ESI 0x0001:0001:0001:0001:0001 (Host-D's dual-home). In State M, Leaf-3 is VXLAN-only and Leaf-4 is dual-encap. Both must advertise the ES route; DF election runs on the union of all ES route advertisers regardless of encap.
  • The per-ES Type 1 route carries the ES Label (VXLAN side: MPLS label field repurposed; Tunnel Encap type 8, RFC 9012) or the ES SID (SRv6 side: BGP Prefix-SID attr RFC 8669 with SRv6 L2 Service TLV type 6 sub-TLV, RFC 9252). Both must consistently identify the same Ethernet Segment.
  • Split-horizon must give consistent results across encaps. The ES Label (VXLAN) and ES SID (SRv6) serve the same function — preventing replication of BUM traffic back into the ES. The gateway must enforce split-horizon using the correct identifier for each encap plane it participates in.
  • DF election algorithm (per RFC 7432 modulo or preference-based) must produce the same designated forwarder result for both encap planes. A topology where Leaf-3 is DF for VXLAN and Leaf-4 is DF for SRv6 on the same ES is valid but must be intentional.
  • Verify mass-withdraw behaviour on ES link failure: the withdrawing leaf must send Type 1/Type 4 withdrawals on both encap planes it participates in, not just one.

12. Gateway Design — Border-Leaf vs Spine-as-Gateway vs Dedicated DCI

The gateway is where two data planes touch. Placement determines blast radius, scale, and rollback flexibility.

Every dual-encap migration needs at least one node that simultaneously speaks VXLAN and SRv6 and stitches routes between the two domains. Where you place that node is a design decision, not an afterthought. The three canonical placements each carry different trade-offs in hardware requirements, fault isolation, and operational complexity. This guide's reference topology pins the gateway on Leaf-2 and Leaf-4 — a redundant border-leaf pair — which is the right default for single-fabric migrations.

Border-leaf as gateway

  • Most common deployment for single-fabric migrations. One or two leaf nodes are configured for dual-encap; they stitch VXLAN and SRv6 at the data plane and re-originate EVPN routes across the encap boundary at the control plane.
  • Blast radius is tightly scoped: a mis-configuration or software defect on the gateway leaf affects only traffic that traverses the encap boundary — pure-VXLAN flows between non-gateway leaves are unaffected.
  • Easy to add or remove during migration: promote a leaf to gateway when ready, demote it back when the migration phase is complete or needs rollback. No changes to the spine or other leaves required.
  • Supports incremental scale-out: add a third border-leaf to the gateway pool without re-architecting the fabric.
  • This guide's default. Leaf-2 and Leaf-4 serve as the redundant border-leaf gateway pair in State M (see §3/§10).

Spine as gateway

  • Fewer physical devices to upgrade: if spines already run a capable NOS, promoting them to gateway duty avoids adding new leaf hardware.
  • Critical caution: spines are throughput-optimised, not feature-rich. Confirm the ASIC and NOS version support SRv6 uSID encap/decap at line rate before committing. Some high-density spine ASICs deliberately omit MPLS/SRv6 to maximise forwarding capacity.
  • Couples gateway function to the fabric backbone: a gateway software defect or config error on a spine can split the fabric, not just the encap boundary. Blast radius is significantly wider than border-leaf placement.
  • Suitable when the spine is already the inter-VRF routing point (collapsed leaf/spine) and the hardware is verified SRv6-capable.
  • DF election and multi-homing interact awkwardly with spine-as-gateway: spines typically do not participate in ESI-LAG, so multi-homed segments can't terminate on them directly.

Dedicated DCI router

  • Designed for inter-fabric or multi-site deployments where the two encap domains are in different data centres or administrative domains.
  • Can be a different vendor or platform than the leaves — the DCI router only needs to speak BGP EVPN with both domains; the underlay is independent on each side.
  • Clean separation: the DCI router is not a member of either fabric's underlay topology. A failure is bounded to the inter-site path, not the intra-fabric forwarding plane.
  • Adds an extra forwarding hop (ingress leaf → DCI router → egress leaf across sites). Latency impact is usually negligible in WAN context but must be accounted for in same-DC designs.
  • Operationally distinct from the fabric: separate change windows, separate NOS image lifecycle. Good for large-scale or mixed-vendor environments; overkill for a contained single-fabric migration.

Hardware checklist

  • The data-plane requirement is the gating constraint. The gateway node must perform VXLAN encap/decap AND SRv6 uSID encap/decap at line rate simultaneously. Verify this against the ASIC's forwarding pipeline — not just the NOS feature list.
  • VXLAN decap + SRv6 encap (VXLAN→SRv6 direction) and SRv6 decap + VXLAN encap (SRv6→VXLAN direction) must both be supported in the same forwarding pass. Some ASICs support one direction in hardware and the other in recirculation (software assist) — this causes asymmetric throughput and is usually unacceptable for production traffic.
  • Route-type translation (re-originating Type 2/Type 5 with different encap attributes) is a control-plane operation on any modern EVPN router. CPU and BGP table scale matter, but this is not a hardware bottleneck.
  • Check TCAM resources: dual-encap doubles the prefix scale requirement in certain ASIC architectures. A 256k-entry TCAM shared between VXLAN and SRv6 routes may exhaust sooner than expected.
  • Validate SRv6 uSID support specifically — not just SRv6. Several platforms support SRv6 with 128-bit SIDs but not uSID (compressed 32-bit block format). The migration uses uSID (fc00:0::/32 block).

HA design

  • Anycast next-hop (preferred): Leaf-2 and Leaf-4 both advertise routes with the same anycast gateway MAC/IP. Ingress leaves ECMP across both. On failure of one gateway, traffic re-hashes to the surviving node with no control-plane reconvergence required on the ingress side.
  • Active/standby with state sync: One gateway is primary; the standby mirrors MAC/ARP/ND tables and takes over on failure. Lower hardware utilisation under normal operation; reconvergence on failover depends on state-sync fidelity and BGP update propagation time.
  • Mid-flow behaviour on gateway failure: VXLAN→SRv6 flows in flight are dropped for the duration of the encap boundary being unavailable. Anycast minimises this to the hash-recalculation time (~sub-second). Active/standby introduces a convergence gap (typically 1–3 seconds depending on BFD timers).
  • This guide's redundant pair (Leaf-2 + Leaf-4) uses anycast: both leaves are configured with the same anycast SVI MAC and participate in the same L3 VNI / SRv6 EVI, so ingress leaves naturally ECMP across both.
  • Configure BFD between ingress leaves and the gateway pair (or use fast BGP timers) to detect gateway failure quickly and trigger ECMP rehash before application-layer timeouts fire.

Three Placements Compared

Border-leaf

  • Best fit: single-fabric migration (this guide's default)
  • Blast radius scoped to cross-encap traffic only
  • Easy promote/demote lifecycle; rollback is leaf-local
  • ECMP across gateway pair (anycast) is straightforward
  • Requires two leaves with dual-encap ASIC support
  • Does not scale to multi-site without DCI augmentation

Spine-as-gateway

  • Best fit: collapsed fabric with verified SRv6-capable spines
  • Fewer devices to upgrade if spines already qualify
  • Spine failure impacts entire fabric, not just encap boundary
  • ASIC feature support must be explicitly verified — throughput ASICs often lack SRv6
  • Multi-homing and ESI-LAG do not terminate cleanly on spines
  • Higher operational risk; not recommended unless spine already serves L3 gateway role

Dedicated DCI

  • Best fit: multi-site or inter-DC; mixed-vendor environments
  • Clean fault isolation — DCI failure bounded to inter-site path
  • Vendor-agnostic: only BGP EVPN peering required with each domain
  • Extra forwarding hop adds latency (acceptable in WAN, evaluate in same-DC)
  • Separate operational lifecycle from fabric (separate NOS, change windows)
  • Overkill for a single contained fabric migration

13. Route-Type Stitching Mechanics

The gateway is a stitching / re-origination PE — intra-fabric, not an ASBR. Same AS, encap boundary.

The gateway is functionally an EVPN re-origination PE within the same AS. The term "ASBR" implies an inter-AS boundary and does NOT apply here — the gateway sits at an encapsulation boundary, not an autonomous-system boundary. Route-type translation is purely a control-plane operation: the gateway withdraws the route received on the VXLAN side and re-originates a new NLRI with its own next-hop and SRv6-specific attributes into the SRv6 BGP-EVPN domain (and vice versa).

Every EVPN route type carries encap-specific payload in its BGP attributes. Stitching means the gateway terminates the incoming BGP session, inspects those attributes, translates the encap-specific fields, rewrites the next-hop to itself, and re-advertises the route into the far domain. RD and RT values are preserved unless an explicit export/import policy rewrites them for domain separation. The five EVPN route types each require a different attribute translation — Type 3 is the most operationally significant because it governs BUM replication membership.

The stitching pattern

  • Receive: The gateway has a BGP-EVPN session toward the VXLAN domain and imports routes matching the configured import RT into a local RIB.
  • Translate: Per-route-type logic rewrites the encap-specific attributes (BGP Encapsulation Extended Community / VNI label field ↔ BGP Prefix-SID + SRv6 Service TLV) and installs the translated route in a re-origination table.
  • Re-originate: The gateway advertises the re-originated route into the SRv6 BGP-EVPN domain with its own loopback as next-hop. The route appears to SRv6 PEs as if the gateway were a native SRv6 PE.
  • Symmetry: The same three-step process runs in the SRv6→VXLAN direction simultaneously. Both directions share the same gateway box — it is two logical PEs sharing one forwarding ASIC.

The Generic Stitching Pipeline

1
Receive route in encap A's BGP-EVPN domain

The gateway's VXLAN-side BGP session receives the NLRI. Attributes present: BGP Encapsulation Extended Community (RFC 9012, type 8) carrying the VXLAN encap indicator; VNI encoded in the MPLS Label field of the NLRI. RD/RT as advertised by the originating VXLAN PE. Next-hop = originating PE's VTEP IP.

2
Translate per-route-type attributes (swap VNI/label for SID, rewrite next-hop)

The gateway applies per-route-type translation logic (detailed below). The BGP Encapsulation Extended Community is replaced by the BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) carrying an SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252) with the appropriate service SID sub-TLV (type 5 for L3 / type 6 for L2). The gateway's own SRv6 loopback becomes the new BGP next-hop. VNI/label values are discarded; the service SID (uDT2U, uDT2M, uDT4/uDT6, or ES SID) encodes the equivalent service identity in the SRv6 domain.

3
Re-originate into encap B's BGP-EVPN domain

The translated NLRI is advertised into the SRv6-side BGP-EVPN domain. SRv6 PEs receive a route that looks like it was originated by a native SRv6 PE — next-hop is the gateway's SRv6 loopback, the Prefix-SID attribute is present, and the SRv6 Service TLV specifies the correct service SID. The RT exported on the re-originated route must match the import RT configured on SRv6 PEs for the target EVI/VRF; RD is rewritten to the gateway's own RD for the re-originated route.

Type 1 (Ethernet A-D) stitching

  • VXLAN side: ESI label encoded in MPLS Label field; BGP Encapsulation EC type 8 (RFC 9012). Two sub-types: per-EVI (Ethernet A-D per EVI) and per-ES (Ethernet A-D per ES).
  • SRv6 side: ES SID replaces the ESI label; BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) + SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252) type 6 sub-TLV (L2 service). The ES SID is a local SID allocated per ESI on the gateway.
  • Aliasing semantics preserved: The per-EVI A-D route advertised by the gateway allows SRv6 PEs to perform aliasing (load-balance toward the ES even when a Type 2 was learned from a different PE). The gateway re-originates per-EVI A-D routes for each ESI it has stitched.
  • Mass-withdrawal: When the gateway receives a mass-withdrawal (per-ES A-D withdraw with ESI label 0), it propagates the withdrawal into the SRv6 domain using the same semantic. SRv6 PEs flush MAC entries for that ESI without needing per-MAC withdrawals — the semantics survive the encap translation.

Type 2 (MAC/IP) stitching

  • VXLAN side: L2 VNI in MPLS Label field 1; L3 VNI (if IP present) in Label field 2; BGP Encapsulation EC type 8.
  • SRv6 side: uDT2U service SID in the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252, type 6 sub-TLV). The uDT2U SID encodes L2 unicast forwarding for the EVI. If an IP route is also present (MAC+IP route), an additional SRv6 Service TLV carrying a uDT4 or uDT6 SID (type 5 sub-TLV) may be included for the L3 component.
  • MAC unchanged: The MAC address in the NLRI is carried verbatim across the encap boundary — it identifies the same host on both sides.
  • MAC mobility sequence number: The MAC Mobility Extended Community and its sequence number are preserved exactly across the translation. The gateway must not reset or increment the sequence number — doing so would trigger unnecessary MAC flushes on SRv6 PEs. The sequence number is a property of the MAC entry, not the encap.
  • Local preference and MED: Retained unless overridden by explicit policy. Preserve them to avoid unintentional traffic-engineering changes across the encap boundary.

Type 3 (IMET) stitching

  • VXLAN side: PMSI Tunnel attribute (RFC 6514) present; tunnel type typically Ingress Replication (type 6) with the VTEP's IP in the Tunnel Identifier field; VNI in the MPLS label field of the PMSI attribute. BGP Encapsulation EC type 8.
  • SRv6 side: PMSI Tunnel attribute (RFC 6514) is rewritten — tunnel type remains Ingress Replication, but the Tunnel Identifier is replaced with the gateway's SRv6 loopback, and the MPLS label field carries the uDT2M service SID. BGP Encapsulation EC is replaced with the BGP Prefix-SID attribute carrying the SRv6 Service TLV (type 6 sub-TLV, uDT2M).
  • Replication list recomputed: SRv6 PEs build their BUM replication list for the EVI from all received Type 3 routes. The gateway's re-originated Type 3 represents the entire VXLAN domain behind it — SRv6 PEs send BUM traffic to the gateway, which then replicates it into the VXLAN domain using the VTEP-side PMSI attribute.
  • VXLAN→SRv6 BUM path: VXLAN PEs replicate BUM to the gateway's VTEP IP. The gateway receives, decapsulates, and re-encapsulates for each SRv6 PE in the EVI's SRv6-side replication list. This is the IR-to-IR bridging case — no multicast underlay required on either side.

Type 4 (ES route) stitching

  • VXLAN side: ESI in the NLRI; DF Election Extended Community present; BGP Encapsulation EC type 8 with ESI label.
  • SRv6 side: ES SID replaces the ESI label in the re-originated Type 4; BGP Prefix-SID attribute (RFC 8669) with SRv6 Service TLV type 6 sub-TLV. DF Election Extended Community is carried across verbatim.
  • DF election on combined view: SRv6 PEs run DF election across all Type 4 routes they receive for an ESI, including the gateway's re-originated route. The outcome is consistent as long as the ESI and DF Election community values are preserved faithfully.
  • Gateway is NOT a DF candidate: The gateway does not originate Type 4 routes for ESIs it is stitching — it only re-originates Type 4 routes from the VXLAN PEs. The gateway itself does not have a multi-homed segment on that ESI; inserting itself as a DF candidate would corrupt the election for the actual multi-homed PEs.

Type 5 (IP Prefix) stitching

  • VXLAN side: IP prefix in NLRI; GW IP and GW MAC fields present (or zero for pure L3VPN semantics); L3 VNI in MPLS Label field; BGP Encapsulation EC type 8.
  • SRv6 side: Simplest translation of all five route types. GW IP is rewritten to the gateway's own IP; GW MAC is rewritten to the gateway's MAC (or zeroed if not needed). L3 VNI label is replaced by uDT4 (IPv4) or uDT6 (IPv6) service SID in the SRv6 Service TLV (RFC 9252, type 5 sub-TLV = L3 service).
  • Route target carries over: The VRF RT must match on both sides for the prefix to be imported into the correct VRF on SRv6 PEs. If the VXLAN and SRv6 domains use different RT assignment schemes, the gateway's export policy must remap the RT at re-origination time.
  • No PMSI, no ES involvement: Type 5 has no BUM or multi-homing attributes — the translation is attribute-for-attribute and straightforward relative to Types 1–4.

14. ESI + MAC Mobility + ARP/ND Across Encaps

The operationally subtle hazards live here — not in single-flow forwarding, but in events.

Single-flow forwarding across the gateway is mechanical. The hazards emerge in events: a host moves, a link fails, an ARP cache goes stale, or an anycast gateway MAC drifts. Each event crosses the encap boundary carrying state that the gateway must preserve or coordinate — and each has a distinct failure mode if it does not.

ESI consistency across encaps

  • An Ethernet Segment spanning VXLAN leaves and SRv6 leaves — or spanning leaves on both sides of the gateway — MUST advertise the same ESI value on both encap domains. The ESI is a 10-byte identifier assigned by the operator (or auto-derived from LACP system ID); it is not invented or modified by the gateway.
  • If the ESI value differs between the VXLAN-side Type 1/4 routes and the SRv6-side re-originated routes, DF election on the SRv6 domain will treat them as unrelated segments, producing incorrect DF results and potentially dual-active flooding where only one forwarder was intended.
  • In the reference topology, Host-D is dual-homed to Leaf-3 (SRv6) and Leaf-4 (gateway-adjacent). The ESI assigned to Host-D's bond interface must be identical in the SRv6-side Type 4 origination and the VXLAN-side re-originated Type 4 — the gateway carries it verbatim (see §13 Type 4 stitching). Verify with show bgp l2vpn evpn route-type ethernet-segment esi <value> on both sides.
  • Auto-derived ESIs from LACP system IDs are stable as long as LACP system priority and system MAC do not change. Any bond reconfiguration on Host-D that changes these values generates a new ESI and must be coordinated across all leaves simultaneously.

MAC mobility events

  • When a host moves from a VXLAN leaf to an SRv6 leaf, the new leaf originates a Type 2 MAC/IP route carrying a MAC Mobility Extended Community with an incremented sequence number (seq = N+1). All PEs that receive a Type 2 for the same MAC with a higher sequence number withdraw their lower-sequence entry — this is the standard RFC 7432 MAC mobility mechanism.
  • The gateway MUST propagate the sequence number from the incoming Type 2 into the re-originated Type 2 on the far domain. If the gateway resets the sequence number to zero (e.g., due to a misconfigured re-origination policy that strips the MAC Mobility EC), PEs on the VXLAN side see seq=0 against the existing seq=N entry and do not withdraw — the old entry persists alongside the new one, causing a MAC flap loop between the two paths.
  • The converse direction applies equally: a host moving from SRv6 Leaf-3 to VXLAN Leaf-1 generates seq=N+1 on the VXLAN side; the gateway re-originates into the SRv6 domain with seq=N+1. SRv6 PEs flush the old SRv6-originated entry.
  • The gateway is never the originator of a MAC mobility event — it is a passive carrier of the sequence number. The sequence number is a property of the MAC entry's ownership, not of the encapsulation.

ARP/ND across the encap boundary

  • ARP-suppression caches on VXLAN PEs and ND-suppression caches on SRv6 PEs must remain coherent across the gateway. A cache hit on the wrong side of the boundary (stale entry pointing to a host that has moved) produces silent unicast black-holing — traffic is encapsulated to the old leaf, not flooded, so the failure is invisible until the cache TTL expires.
  • When a host on the VXLAN side ARPs for a host on the SRv6 side, the gateway proxies the ARP reply using the MAC/IP binding it learned from the SRv6-side Type 2 route. Proxy-ARP is only safe if the gateway's MAC/IP table is current — a stale Type 2 (e.g., not yet withdrawn after a host move) causes the gateway to proxy the wrong MAC.
  • If proxy-ARP is not configured or not safe (e.g., the IP is shared across multiple MACs via HSRP/VRRP), the gateway falls back to flooding the ARP request into the far bridge domain and forwarding the response back — this is correct but adds latency and BUM load on the gateway.
  • For IPv6, the equivalent is Neighbor Solicitation proxying across the encap boundary. The same coherence requirement applies; NS/NA flooding fallback works identically to ARP.
  • Keep Type 2 route TTLs and hold-timers consistent on both sides of the gateway. A VXLAN PE with a shorter MAC aging timer will withdraw Type 2 routes before the SRv6 side does, leaving a stale proxy-ARP entry on the gateway. Align BGP route hold-times and MAC aging across both domains.

Symmetric IRB consistency

  • Both encap domains use symmetric IRB (§8) with an anycast gateway IP + MAC per EVI. The anycast gateway MAC and IP must be identical on all PEs across both encaps — VXLAN leaves and SRv6 leaves must advertise the same anycast gateway MAC for a given EVI.
  • If the anycast gateway MAC drifts (e.g., the VXLAN leaves use one MAC and SRv6 leaves use another), hosts that move across the boundary learn a different gateway MAC after the move. The old gateway MAC entry ages out of the host's ARP cache at the platform default (often 4 hours), and until then the host sends L2 frames toward the wrong MAC, which are dropped. The symptom is intermittent connectivity after a host move, resolving only after ARP cache expiry.
  • The gateway itself uses its own L3 interface MAC for inter-subnet routing between the two domains — this is distinct from the anycast gateway MAC used intra-domain. Do not confuse the two: the anycast gateway MAC is a per-EVI shared value; the gateway's own MAC is its router interface MAC used for inter-domain traffic only.
  • Audit anycast gateway MAC consistency with show bgp l2vpn evpn route-type mac-ip filtering on the gateway IP — all Type 2 routes for that IP should carry the same MAC across all PEs in both domains.

A MAC Mobility Event Across the Gateway

1
Host was on VXLAN Leaf-1 — seq=N in both domains

Host-A's MAC is active on VXLAN Leaf-1. Leaf-1 has originated a Type 2 MAC/IP route with MAC Mobility Extended Community seq=N. The gateway has re-originated this into the SRv6 domain with seq=N (carried verbatim). All SRv6 PEs, including Leaf-3 and Leaf-4, have installed the route pointing toward the gateway → Leaf-1 path. VXLAN PEs have installed the Type 2 locally as a normal intra-VXLAN entry.

2
Host moves to SRv6 Leaf-3 — Leaf-3 originates Type 2 seq=N+1

Host-A connects to SRv6 Leaf-3. Leaf-3 detects the MAC (via data-plane learning or gratuitous ARP) and originates a new Type 2 MAC/IP route with MAC Mobility EC seq=N+1. This route is advertised into the SRv6 BGP-EVPN domain with Leaf-3's SRv6 loopback as next-hop and a uDT2U service SID. All SRv6 PEs with a higher-sequence route now prefer Leaf-3 as the MAC owner.

3
Gateway sees both old (VXLAN, seq=N) and new (SRv6, seq=N+1) Type 2 routes

The gateway holds the re-originated VXLAN-side Type 2 (seq=N) and has now received Leaf-3's SRv6-side Type 2 (seq=N+1). Per RFC 7432, seq=N+1 wins. The gateway withdraws its previously re-originated VXLAN-side Type 2 from the VXLAN domain and re-originates Leaf-3's Type 2 (next-hop = gateway's VTEP IP, VNI encoded, seq=N+1 MAC Mobility EC preserved) into the VXLAN BGP-EVPN domain.

4
VXLAN PEs receive withdrawal + new Type 2 seq=N+1 pointing to gateway

VXLAN Leaf-1 receives the withdrawal of its own Type 2 reflected back (or sees the higher-seq Type 2 from the gateway) and flushes its local MAC entry for Host-A. All VXLAN PEs install the new Type 2 pointing to the gateway's VTEP IP as next-hop. Traffic toward Host-A from the VXLAN domain now flows: VXLAN PE → gateway (VXLAN decap) → SRv6 encap to Leaf-3.

5
Both domains converge — old entry withdrawn, forwarding is clean

Leaf-1's VXLAN-side Type 2 (seq=N) is withdrawn from the VXLAN domain (Leaf-1 no longer owns the MAC). The gateway's re-originated entry is the only VXLAN-side advertisement for Host-A's MAC. SRv6 domain already has Leaf-3's direct Type 2 (seq=N+1). Forwarding in both directions is consistent: SRv6→Host-A goes directly to Leaf-3; VXLAN→Host-A goes through the gateway to Leaf-3. No MAC flap, no duplicate entries — because seq=N+1 was preserved at every hop.

Three most common operational pitfalls in this section:
  1. MAC flap loop from sequence-number reset at the gateway. A re-origination policy that strips or zeroes the MAC Mobility Extended Community causes the re-originated Type 2 to carry seq=0. PEs on the far side see seq=0 competing with the existing seq=N entry; neither wins definitively, and both entries oscillate. The symptom is a rapid MAC flap log on multiple PEs simultaneously, resolving only when one side withdraws. Fix: verify the re-origination policy explicitly passes through the MAC Mobility EC unchanged — do not rely on default pass-through; test with a deliberate host-move in a lab before production cutover.
  2. Duplicate-address / DAD storms from incoherent ARP-suppression caches. When a host moves from one encap domain to the other, the old ARP-suppression cache entry on the origin domain's PEs may persist beyond the BGP withdrawal (cache TTL outlasts the BGP convergence event). During that window, a new host connecting with the same IP triggers proxy-ARP responses from both the origin PE (stale cache) and the gateway (fresh Type 2). The result is two ARP replies with different MACs for the same IP — the host's ARP cache flips between them, and IPv6 hosts may trigger DAD storms. Fix: align ARP/ND cache aging timers with BGP hold-down timers; flush ARP caches on the origin PEs as part of the host-move procedure; do not set cache TTLs longer than the BGP session hold-time.
  3. Silent-host visibility gaps when a quiet host is only known to one encap. A host that has not sent or received traffic for an extended period may have its Type 2 route aged out by MAC aging on its local leaf. If the host is only reachable from the far encap domain (e.g., an SRv6 host that has been silent), VXLAN-side PEs have no Type 2 entry and no proxy-ARP mapping. An ARP from a VXLAN host is flooded to the gateway, which floods it into the SRv6 BUM replication list — but if the SRv6 leaf's ARP-suppression cache has also aged out, the solicitation must reach the host directly to get a response. If the host is in a deep power-save state or has a restrictive host firewall, no response is generated, and the VXLAN host concludes the destination is unreachable. Fix: set MAC aging timers conservatively (longer than the expected quiescent period for silent hosts), or use gratuitous-ARP refresh mechanisms on the host side; monitor for Type 2 route counts per EVI on both sides of the gateway and alert on unexpected drops.
15. Gateway Packet Walks — VXLAN Host ↔ SRv6 Host

A packet that crosses the encap boundary. Forward + return.

Host-A (on VXLAN-only Leaf-1) and Host-B (on SRv6-only Leaf-3) are in the same VNI-10100 / VLAN-100 segment. Neither leaf can reach the other: Leaf-1 speaks VXLAN exclusively; Leaf-3 speaks SRv6 exclusively. Gateway Leaf-2 is the dual-encap border-leaf (State M) that bridges the two data planes — it decapsulates inbound frames and re-encapsulates them in the far domain's format. Every cross-domain packet transits Leaf-2 in both directions.

Scenario

  • VNI 10100 is provisioned on Leaf-1 (VXLAN), Leaf-2 (gateway, both encaps), and Leaf-3 (SRv6).
  • Leaf-1 only understands VXLAN; Leaf-3 only understands SRv6 uSID. Neither side can directly reach the other.
  • Gateway Leaf-2 has re-originated Leaf-3's Type 2 routes into the VXLAN domain (next-hop = Leaf-2's VTEP 203.0.113.2). Leaf-1 therefore resolves Host-B's MAC via the gateway, not via Leaf-3 directly.
  • Symmetrically, Leaf-2 has re-originated Leaf-1's Type 2 routes into the SRv6 domain (next-hop = Leaf-2's uDT2U SID fc00:0:1:2:e000::). Leaf-3 resolves Host-A's MAC via the gateway.
  • Both directions require decap + re-encap at Leaf-2 — two encap operations per direction, six per round-trip.

Each direction requires the gateway to execute two encap operations back-to-back: a decap of the incoming format followed immediately by a re-encap in the outgoing format. Across a round-trip, that is six encap operations total. This is not latency-free — each operation is a separate ASIC pipeline pass on most switching silicon.

What the walks expose

  • 6 encap operations per round-trip. Decap + re-encap at the gateway in each direction. The gateway is the most latency-sensitive node in the path; size ASIC pipeline throughput accordingly, and avoid daisy-chaining multiple gateways.
  • MTU at the gateway must cover both encap formats. The gateway must handle VXLAN frames (50 B overhead) arriving and SRv6 frames (54 B overhead) arriving. Its fabric-facing interfaces must tolerate jumbo frames up to inner MTU + 54 B; if the gateway also generates SRv6 frames with an SRH (multi-segment path), the overhead is larger — always size for the worst case. A gateway configured for 9150-byte fabric MTU with 9100-byte inner frames leaves only 50 B of headroom — too tight for SRv6.
  • Asymmetric-routing risk. Forward and return paths SHOULD traverse the same gateway instance. If an anycast gateway pair exists (Leaf-2 + Leaf-4 both in State M), misconfigured ECMP policy can route the forward flow through Leaf-2 and the return flow through Leaf-4. Stateful middleboxes (firewalls, load-balancers, DPI) that inspect both directions of a TCP session will see only half the conversation and either drop or mis-track the flow. Pin forward and return to the same gateway using consistent hashing on the five-tuple, or use flow-based ECMP with sticky affinity across the gateway pair.
Always test forward AND return through the same gateway before per-service migration. Asymmetric paths — forward via Leaf-2, return via Leaf-4 — are a common misconfiguration that is invisible to a one-directional ping test but breaks stateful services (firewalls, load-balancers, NAT) immediately under real traffic. Verify with a bidirectional flow tool (e.g., iperf3 or a stateful TCP probe) and confirm gateway ASIC counters increment on the same node for both directions before signing off on the migration.
16. Migration Order & Approach

The order isn't arbitrary — some services carry less state, less risk, and roll back more cleanly.

Start where failure is cheapest: pure L3 services carry no bridge-domain state, have clean per-VRF rollback, and expose routing correctness without risking MAC/ARP stability. Work toward services that carry more per-host state (IRB, L2) and finally ESI multi-homing and DCI, where dual-encap overlap must be tightly controlled.

Recommended order

  • 1 — L3 / Type 5 (lowest risk): No bridge-domain state; per-VRF rollback is atomic. Advertise via SRv6, verify routing table, then withdraw VXLAN routes. A mis-step affects only routed prefixes, never MAC tables.
  • 2 — IRB: Adds anycast gateway state (MAC + IP must be consistent on both encaps). Migrate after L3 VRFs are proven; symmetric-IRB cutover is clean once the gateway is validated in §13/§14.
  • 3 — L2 single-homed: Introduces per-host MAC/ARP state. VNI-to-service-SID mapping must be verified before cutover; ARP-suppression cache must be flushed post-cutover to prevent stale entries.
  • 4 — L2 multi-homed / ESI (highest per-service risk): DF election must stay stable across the encap-A-only → dual-encap → encap-B-only transition. ES routes must be present on both encaps before switchover. Any DF flap during transition causes transient traffic loss on the multihomed segment.
  • 5 — DCI (last): Inter-site connectivity is cut over after all intra-site services are validated on SRv6. DCI is treated as a migratable unit here even though §11 frames it as a deployment-topology cross-cut — the inter-site cutover is its own discrete step with its own pre-checks.

Cutover patterns

  • Per-VRF: Cleanest scope — migrate one VRF at a time. Failure blast-radius is a single tenant's routed traffic. Best starting point.
  • Per-VLAN: Within a tenant, migrate one VLAN/VNI at a time. Useful when a tenant spans many VLANs; lets you validate L2 state service-by-service.
  • Per-tenant: Groups all VRFs and VLANs for a tenant into a single cutover window. Higher blast-radius but minimises dual-encap overlap duration for that tenant.
  • Per-leaf-group: Migrate all tenants on a set of leaves together. Suited to hardware refresh (leaf replacement); the new hardware comes up SRv6-only, removing the need for a dual-encap state machine.

Rollback design

  • Leave VXLAN configuration in place on each leaf until every tenant on that leaf is validated on SRv6. Do not remove VXLAN config leaf-by-leaf as you go — rollback requires instantaneous re-activation.
  • Per-service rollback must not require gateway reconfiguration. The gateway holds both encap contexts throughout the migration; per-leaf or per-tenant rollback is achieved by toggling route advertisements, not by touching gateway state. This is a hard invariant from §13/§14.
  • Maintain a rollback timer: if validation is not passed within the agreed window (e.g., 30 minutes), roll back automatically. Do not leave dual-encap state active indefinitely.

Observability during cutover

  • MAC-table convergence: Watch MAC-table size on the gateway and target leaf both before and after cutover. A drop in MAC count on the SRv6 leaf immediately post-cutover indicates ARP/ND has not re-populated; allow convergence time (typically <30 s) before declaring failure.
  • ARP-table consistency: Compare ARP tables on the gateway (both encap sides) and on the target leaf. Stale ARP entries pointing to the old encap cause silent black-holes — not drops, but wrong-path forwarding. Flush ARP-suppression cache on the leaf after L2 cutover.
  • Gateway counters: Monitor VXLAN decap, SRv6 decap, and SRv6 encap counters on the gateway. During dual-encap, both VXLAN and SRv6 counters should increment. After cutover, only SRv6 encap/decap should be active for the migrated service; residual VXLAN counter increments are a rollback signal.
  • Expected pattern: VXLAN counters ramp down, SRv6 counters ramp up over the convergence window (<60 s for most topologies). Anomalous pattern: both counters flat-line (routing black-hole) or SRv6 counters stay at zero (re-origination not active on the gateway).

Recommended Migration Order

1
L3 / Type 5 — pure routed VRFs

No bridge-domain state. Per-VRF rollback is atomic. Lowest risk; start here to validate SRv6 routing end-to-end before touching any L2 services.

2
IRB — anycast gateway segments

Adds anycast MAC/IP state. Requires consistent gateway MAC/IP on both encap domains. Migrate after L3 VRFs are validated; leverage §13/§14 gateway invariants.

3
L2 single-homed — bridged VNIs, single uplink

Introduces per-host MAC/ARP state. VNI↔service-SID mapping must be verified; ARP-suppression cache must be flushed after cutover. Moderate risk.

4
L2 multi-homed / ESI — dual-homed CE/server

Trickiest: DF election must stay stable across encap-A-only → dual-encap → encap-B-only. ES routes must be present on both encaps before cutover. Any DF flap causes transient loss.

5
DCI — inter-site connectivity

Treated as a migratable unit: the discrete inter-site cutover step. Cut over after all intra-site services are validated on SRv6. Site-by-site or gateway-to-gateway depending on inter-site fabric design.

Cutover Patterns

per-VRF
One VRF at a time. Smallest blast-radius. Best starting pattern for L3 and IRB services. Failure isolates to a single tenant's routed traffic.
per-VLAN
Within a tenant: one VLAN/VNI per cutover window. Validates L2 MAC/ARP state service-by-service. Good for tenants with many VLANs.
per-tenant
All VRFs and VLANs for a tenant in one window. Higher blast-radius but minimises time spent in dual-encap for that tenant.
per-leaf-group
All tenants on a set of leaves cut over together. Ideal for hardware refresh — new hardware comes up SRv6-only, eliminating the dual-encap state machine entirely.
Invariant: every per-service cutover must be reversible without touching the gateway. The gateway holds both encap contexts throughout the full migration window. Per-leaf and per-tenant rollback is achieved by toggling route advertisements on the leaf, not by reconfiguring the gateway. Any cutover procedure that requires gateway reconfiguration to roll back violates this invariant and must be redesigned.

L3 / Type 5 cutover

  • Pre-check: Record VRF count and prefix count per VRF on both VXLAN and SRv6 sides. Verify the gateway is re-originating Type 5 routes in both directions (check BGP RIB on gateway). Confirm MTU on SRv6 fabric-facing interfaces.
  • Cutover: On the target leaf, enable SRv6 VRF advertisement for the selected VRF. Verify the SRv6 Type 5 route appears in the BGP RIB on remote SRv6 peers. Once confirmed, withdraw the VXLAN Type 5 advertisement for that VRF.
  • Validation: Prefix count on SRv6 peers equals pre-cutover VXLAN count. Probe traffic (ICMP + TCP) from an SRv6-only leaf to an SRv6-only leaf through the now-migrated VRF. Check gateway VXLAN encap counters drop to zero for that VRF; SRv6 encap counters active.
  • Rollback: Re-advertise the VXLAN Type 5 routes for the VRF. Withdraw SRv6 advertisement. No gateway reconfiguration required — gateway continues to hold both encap contexts. Convergence typically <BFD timer + 1 BGP scan cycle.

IRB cutover

  • Pre-check: Verify anycast gateway MAC and IP are identical on both the VXLAN and SRv6 instances. The gateway must not be a DF candidate (per §14) — confirm this before any cutover. Check that ARP-suppression tables are populated correctly on gateway for the VNI.
  • Cutover: Enable symmetric-IRB on the SRv6 instance of the target VNI/VRF pair. Advertise SRv6 Type 2 (MAC/IP) and Type 5 routes for the IRB segment. Verify routes appear on SRv6 peers. Withdraw VXLAN Type 2 and Type 5 for the same segment.
  • Validation: Host-to-host routed traffic (inter-subnet) flows correctly on SRv6 path. ARP table on SRv6 leaf shows correct MAC/IP bindings. Gateway counters show SRv6 IRB lookups active, VXLAN IRB counters quiescent.
  • Rollback: Re-advertise VXLAN Type 2/5 for the IRB segment. Withdraw SRv6 advertisements. Gateway does not need reconfiguration; rollback is route-advertisement-only. Note: MAC mobility sequence numbers are preserved by the gateway (per §13) — re-advertising VXLAN does not reset mobility counters.

L2 single-homed cutover

  • Pre-check: Verify VNI-to-service-SID mapping is provisioned on the target SRv6 leaf (show evpn evi detail equivalent). Snapshot the MAC table for the VNI on the gateway and the source VXLAN leaf. Record ARP-suppression cache entry count.
  • Cutover: On the target leaf, activate the SRv6 EVI for the VNI. Advertise SRv6 Type 2 routes (MAC/IP). Verify Type 2 routes appear on the gateway and remote SRv6 leaves. Withdraw VXLAN Type 2 routes for the VNI from the source leaf.
  • Validation: MAC table on SRv6 leaf matches pre-cutover snapshot count. Flush ARP-suppression cache on the gateway for that VNI — stale entries pointing to the old VXLAN VTEP will otherwise black-hole traffic silently until ARP refresh. Re-verify MAC/IP bindings post-flush. Test L2 forwarding with a probe frame.
  • Rollback: Re-advertise VXLAN Type 2 routes. Withdraw SRv6 Type 2. Flush ARP-suppression cache again (entries may now have both old and new VTEP). No gateway reconfiguration required.

L2 multi-homed (ESI) cutover

  • Pre-check: Confirm ES (Ethernet Segment) routes (Type 1 and Type 4) are present for the ESI on both VXLAN and the SRv6 instance before starting. Verify DF election state — identify which PE is currently DF for each VLAN on the ESI. Record the DF PE; this must not change during cutover. The gateway must not be a DF candidate (§14 invariant).
  • Transition sequence: This is a three-phase transition to keep DF stable:
    1. Phase A (encap-A-only → dual-encap): Activate SRv6 EVI for the ESI segment. Advertise SRv6 ES (Type 1), DF election (Type 4), and MAC/IP (Type 2) routes. Do NOT withdraw VXLAN ES routes yet. Verify SRv6 DF election converges to the same PE as VXLAN DF. If they diverge, stop and investigate before proceeding.
    2. Phase B (dual-encap steady state): Both VXLAN and SRv6 ES routes active. Traffic can take either path. Verify no MAC flap events in the MAC-table log. Run for a validation window (minimum 5 minutes under traffic).
    3. Phase C (dual-encap → encap-B-only): Withdraw VXLAN ES routes (Type 1 and Type 4) for the ESI. VXLAN DF election is withdrawn; SRv6 DF election takes full ownership. Monitor MAC-table for convergence. Withdraw residual VXLAN Type 2 routes.
  • Validation: DF PE for each VLAN on the ESI is unchanged post-cutover. No MAC mobility counter increments (MAC moves). BFD/LAG state on the multihomed CE/server is stable. End-to-end L2 probes pass.
  • Rollback: Re-advertise VXLAN ES (Type 1, Type 4) routes. Allow VXLAN DF election to reconverge. Withdraw SRv6 ES routes. This is the most complex rollback — allow a full BGP scan cycle for DF re-election before declaring rollback complete. No gateway reconfiguration required.

DCI cutover

  • Framing: DCI is a migratable unit here — the inter-site cutover step — even though §11 frames it as a deployment-topology cross-cut. This playbook covers the case where inter-site connectivity needs to move from VXLAN-based DCI to SRv6-based DCI after all intra-site services are migrated.
  • Pre-check: All intra-site services on both sites must be validated on SRv6 before beginning DCI cutover. Verify inter-site BGP sessions (EVPN AF) are up on both VXLAN and the SRv6 underlay. Confirm the inter-site fabric (DC interconnect / WAN) can carry the SRv6 outer IPv6 header (MTU + IPv6 transit support).
  • Cutover patterns: Two options: site-by-site (migrate one site's inter-site peering to SRv6 at a time, with the gateway bridging encaps at the DCI boundary) or gateway-to-gateway (both border leaves at both sites simultaneously, cleaner but higher risk). Site-by-site is recommended; the DCI gateway boundary mirrors the intra-site gateway pattern.
  • Cutover: Establish SRv6 inter-site EVPN sessions. Advertise inter-site Type 2/5 routes via SRv6. Verify routes appear on both sites' SRv6 RIBs. Withdraw VXLAN inter-site route advertisements. Confirm inter-site traffic flows end-to-end.
  • Validation: Inter-site MAC/IP reachability confirmed via probes. BGP route counts consistent across sites. No MAC mobility events on hosts whose MACs are learned cross-site. Latency/throughput unchanged (SRv6 outer header is 40 B vs. VXLAN's 50 B, slight improvement expected on routed paths).
  • Rollback: Re-activate VXLAN inter-site EVPN sessions. Re-advertise VXLAN inter-site routes. Withdraw SRv6 inter-site advertisements. No intra-site gateway reconfiguration required.

18. Final Cutover, Rollback, Cleanup

The gateway is a crutch. Eventually you remove it.

Once every service has been migrated to SRv6, the dual-encap gateway (Leaf-2/Leaf-4, §3) has done its job. Leaving it in place indefinitely is safe but wasteful — it holds VXLAN config, VTEP loopbacks, and IPv4 underlay state that serve no live traffic. This section covers the ordered process for removing that infrastructure, knowing when rollback is realistic, and what must be validated before you declare the migration complete.

When the gateway can retire

  • All services — L3/Type 5, IRB, L2 single-homed, L2 multi-homed (ESI), and DCI — have been individually validated on SRv6 per the §17 playbooks.
  • Zero VXLAN (encap-A) advertisements remain in any BGP RIB — no Type 2, Type 3, or Type 5 routes with VXLAN encap are being originated.
  • Gateway VXLAN encap counters have been zero-byte for a defined observation window (recommended: 7 days production, minimum 48 hours low-traffic).
  • Observability dashboards (§16) show green for the full window: no MAC mobility events, no BFD flaps, no SRv6 SID resolution failures.
  • A formal sign-off from service owners for each migrated service has been obtained and logged — this creates the audit trail that allows decom to proceed.

Gateway decommissioning

  • Withdraw all remaining VXLAN route advertisements from the gateway (any residual Type 2/3/5 still being originated for completeness). Verify no VXLAN routes appear in any peer's RIB.
  • Confirm traffic on old encap is zero: check gateway encap/decap packet counters on all VXLAN-facing interfaces; wait at least one polling interval (e.g., 5 minutes) to confirm sustained zero.
  • Remove the dual-encap configuration block from the gateway: delete VXLAN EVI instances, VNI-to-service-SID stitching config, and VXLAN-side BGP route-policy attachments. Leave SRv6 config intact — the gateway border leaf can be repurposed.
  • Optionally repurpose Leaf-2/Leaf-4 as a regular SRv6 leaf: remove its "gateway" role in the topology documentation, update naming conventions, and verify it now participates in the SRv6 fabric as a peer (not a translation point).
  • Update all topology diagrams, runbooks, and NMS device roles to reflect the post-migration state.

VXLAN-specific cleanup

  • Remove unused VTEP loopback interfaces on all leaves that previously served as VTEPs — these addresses are no longer reachable or needed once VXLAN is withdrawn.
  • Retire the IPv4 underlay if SRv6 is now the only data plane: remove OSPF/IS-IS IPv4 instances, IPv4 loopback advertisements, and any IPv4-only underlay peerings. Validate that SRv6 forwarding is unaffected (it uses the IPv6 underlay exclusively).
  • Remove PIM/multicast underlay configuration if BUM traffic was originally handled via multicast replication rather than ingress-replication. Multicast groups used for VXLAN BUM serve no purpose once all VNIs are decommissioned.
  • Clean up any VXLAN-specific ACLs, QoS policies, and monitoring agent configurations (SNMP OIDs, streaming telemetry paths) that referenced VXLAN VNIs or VTEP interfaces.

Post-migration validation

  • Run a full end-to-end connectivity test per service: for each VRF/VNI that was migrated, verify reachability between at least two endpoints that previously relied on VXLAN paths.
  • Compare before/after packet-counter baselines and forwarding-table scale: SRv6 SID table sizes, BGP route counts, and FIB entry counts should all be within expected bounds (see §9 for scale planning).
  • Confirm zero VXLAN remnants in all RIBs and FIBs — run show bgp evpn and show fib equivalents on all leaves and verify no VXLAN encap is present anywhere in the forwarding state.
  • Verify SRv6 SID assignments are stable: no SID collisions, no unexpected re-allocations, uSID block usage within planned bounds.
  • Capture and archive the post-migration baseline as the new steady-state reference for future troubleshooting.

What to keep around

  • Retain all dual-stack-period documentation — the migration log, per-service sign-offs, and any incident reports from the transition window — for audit and postmortem purposes. Minimum retention: 12 months or per your change-management policy.
  • Keep the rollback runbook current and accessible: rollback is realistic as long as VXLAN configuration remains archived and can be redeployed. Once deep cleanup (VTEP removal, IPv4 underlay teardown) is complete, rollback becomes a new VXLAN deployment project, not a one-day operation.
  • Archive the VXLAN config snapshots (pre-migration baselines) alongside the rollback runbook; without them, re-deploying VXLAN requires reconstructing configuration from scratch.
  • Schedule a 30-day and 90-day post-migration review: revisit scale numbers, review any incidents, and confirm the SRv6 fabric is operating as designed before closing the migration project.

Decommissioning Sequence

1
Withdraw VXLAN advertisements from gateway

On Leaf-2/Leaf-4, withdraw all remaining VXLAN BGP route advertisements (Type 2, Type 3, Type 5). Verify no VXLAN encap routes appear in any peer's EVPN RIB. Confirm via show bgp evpn on all SRv6 leaves — zero VXLAN next-hops expected.

2
Verify zero VXLAN traffic

Poll gateway VXLAN encap and decap packet counters on all VXLAN-facing interfaces. Wait a full observation interval (minimum 5 minutes; recommended one full polling cycle of your telemetry system). Counters must remain at zero before proceeding — any non-zero count indicates a residual flow that must be traced and migrated.

3
Remove dual-encap config and VTEP interfaces

Delete the VXLAN EVI instances, VNI-to-SID stitching config, and VTEP loopback interfaces from all border leaves. Remove VXLAN-side BGP route-policy attachments. Verify SRv6 operation is unaffected after each deletion (check SRv6 encap counters and BGP session state before proceeding to the next leaf).

4
Retire IPv4 underlay and PIM if unused

If the IPv4 underlay (OSPF/IS-IS IPv4 AF) served VXLAN exclusively, remove it: withdraw IPv4 loopbacks, tear down IPv4 IGP adjacencies, and remove any multicast (PIM) configuration used for BUM replication. Confirm SRv6 forwarding remains stable on the IPv6 underlay — run end-to-end probes across all migrated services before declaring the underlay cleanup complete.

Rollback after deep cleanup is a new project, not a one-day operation. As long as VXLAN configuration is still present on the gateways (before Step 3), rollback means re-advertising routes — feasible in hours. Once VTEP interfaces and the IPv4 underlay are removed (Steps 3–4), restoring VXLAN requires reprovisioning the entire VXLAN data plane from scratch. Keep the archived VXLAN config snapshots and the rollback runbook current until you are certain the migration is permanent and the 90-day post-migration review is closed.
19. Troubleshooting — Control Plane vs Data Plane

The encap changes what you capture and how you read it. The control plane barely moves.

Migrating from VXLAN to SRv6 narrows the troubleshooting surface more than most engineers expect. BGP-EVPN still carries the same route types (Type 2 MAC/IP, Type 3 IMET, Type 5 IP prefix); the control plane commands are identical; the MAC and ARP tables are unchanged. What shifts is the data plane: the forwarding encapsulation token, the capture filter, and where to look for MTU-related drops.

What stays the same

  • show bgp l2vpn evpn — same route types, same attribute fields; only the encap extended community (VXLAN vs SRv6) differs in the attribute block.
  • show evpn mac / show evpn mac detail — MAC-to-next-hop table shape is identical; next-hop value changes from VTEP IP to service SID.
  • show evpn arp / show evpn nd — ARP/ND binding table format unchanged.
  • show ip route vrf <vrf> / show ipv6 route vrf <vrf> — VRF RIB output unchanged; next-hop pointer shifts from VTEP to SID.
  • BGP session state, neighbor flaps, and route count deltas are diagnosed identically on both encaps.

Control-plane diffs per encap

  • VXLAN: BGP EVPN next-hop = VTEP loopback IP; VNI carried in the VXLAN encap extended community (e.g., RT:1:1000, encap:VXLAN, label:10000). Verify with show bgp l2vpn evpn route-type 3 — IMET carries the VTEP IP as the PMSI tunnel endpoint.
  • SRv6: BGP EVPN next-hop = node locator; service SID encoded as a BGP prefix-SID attribute or SRv6 L3VPN/L2VPN SID TLV. Verify with show bgp l2vpn evpn route-type 3 detail — look for the SRv6 SID sub-TLV field.
  • Encap extended community mismatch between two peers is the most common control-plane cause of one-way traffic after partial migration — one side still advertising VXLAN encap to an SRv6-only leaf.
  • Check SID resolution: show segment-routing srv6 sid / show srv6 locator — a service SID without a resolved locator endpoint will drop silently.

Data-plane capture — VXLAN

  • Capture filter: udp port 4789 — VXLAN uses UDP/4789 as the standard destination port.
  • Frame structure: outer Ethernet → outer IP → UDP (dport 4789) → 8-byte VXLAN header (flags + 24-bit VNI) → inner Ethernet frame.
  • VNI is in plaintext in bytes 5–7 of the VXLAN header; readable without decryption. Wireshark dissects automatically with the VXLAN dissector.
  • Identify the source VTEP from the outer source IP; the destination VTEP from the outer destination IP (unicast ingress-replication) or multicast group (PIM BUM).
  • Look for fragmentation at the outer IP layer — any fragment indicates an MTU problem; the inner frame is invisible in non-first fragments.

Data-plane capture — SRv6

  • Capture filter (SRH present): ip6[6] == 43 — IPv6 next-header 43 is the Routing Header; SRH is a Routing Header with type 4.
  • Capture filter (uSID, no SRH): ip6 and ip6[24:4] == 0xfc000001 — match packets whose IPv6 destination falls within the uN locator block (e.g., fc00:0:1::/48 per §9 planning). Adjust the mask to match your deployed locator range.
  • uSID: the active segment function is encoded directly in the destination IPv6 address (bits 48–95 of a 128-bit uSID); no SRH is present for single-segment paths, which is the common case in a uSID fabric.
  • To decode the service function: extract the IPv6 destination, split at the 16-bit uSID boundary, and map each 16-bit block to your SID allocation table (show srv6 sid).
  • No fragmentation on the SRv6 path is permitted (IPv6 forbids in-network fragmentation); an oversized packet is dropped with ICMPv6 Packet Too Big — capture ICMPv6 type 2 as the drop signal.

Gateway-specific failures

  • Stuck routes: a service route present on VXLAN leaves but absent on SRv6 leaves usually means the gateway did not re-originate the Type 5 or Type 2 route with the correct SRv6 encap after the stitching config was applied. Check show bgp l2vpn evpn summary on the gateway — confirm it is advertising to SRv6 peers.
  • One-way traffic: asymmetric encap — SRv6 leaf sends to VXLAN host correctly (via gateway re-encap), but VXLAN leaf's return path bypasses the gateway entirely (e.g., direct Type 2 learned via BGP shortcut). Verify that VXLAN leaves point their return path through the gateway, not a direct SRv6 path they cannot process.
  • State desync after gateway failover: if the gateway (Leaf-2/Leaf-4) fails over to its redundant peer (§3 HA), the new active gateway must re-establish the stitching state before forwarding resumes. Monitor MAC mobility event counters immediately after a failover event — a spike indicates the re-advertisement storm is in progress.
  • Missing IPv6 underlay route: SRv6 traffic silently black-holes if the locator prefix is absent from the underlay (IS-IS SR-IPv6 or BGP-LU not redistributing the locator). Run show isis database detail or show bgp ipv6 unicast to verify locator reachability from the gateway's perspective.

20. Monitoring, Telemetry, Observability

Dashboards and alerts need migrating too — VNI-keyed counters don't exist on SRv6 leaves.

The migration changes the vocabulary of your monitoring stack as much as it changes the network. VXLAN monitoring is VNI-centric: every dashboard panel, alert threshold, and YANG subscription is keyed on a VNI. SRv6 monitoring is SID-centric: the granularity unit is a service SID (or a locator prefix), and VNI-keyed panels go blank the moment VXLAN is withdrawn. Plan the monitoring migration in parallel with the forwarding-plane migration, not after it.

Counter mapping

  • VXLAN: per-VNI encap/decap packet and byte counters; per-VTEP tunnel counters; per-NVE interface aggregate. YANG path example: openconfig-network-instance:network-instances/network-instance[name=*]/vlans/vlan[vlan-id=*]/state/counters.
  • SRv6: per-service-SID counters (function-level); per-locator aggregate (locator-level). YANG paths vary by vendor — look under Cisco-IOS-XR-segment-routing-srv6-oper or openconfig-segment-routing:segment-routing/srv6/locators/locator[name=*]/sids/sid[value=*]/state.
  • Function-level (per-SID) granularity is equivalent to per-VNI granularity; locator-level granularity is coarser (one counter for all services on a node) — ensure your telemetry subscription requests function-level paths to maintain visibility parity.
  • During the dual-stack window, subscribe to both VXLAN VNI paths and SRv6 SID paths simultaneously; plot them in separate panels to track traffic shift without losing either side's visibility.

Flow export

  • sFlow and IPFIX both continue to work on SRv6-encapsulated traffic — the sampling point is still the ingress interface.
  • VXLAN flow records: collector decodes inner flow by stripping the VXLAN header; VNI appears as a tunnel metadata field in IPFIX (Information Element 351: virtualStationInterfaceName or vendor-specific VNI IE).
  • SRv6 flow records: the outer IPv6 header is sampled; the SID appears as the outer destination IP. Collectors must be configured to recognize the SRH extension header (next-header 43) and extract the segment list. Update collector templates to add SRH parsing if absent.
  • Check your flow collector's SRv6 support matrix before cutover — many collectors added SRH support only in recent versions. Upgrade or add a mediator with SRH-aware normalization if needed.
  • Inner flow visibility (tenant IP addresses) is preserved in both encaps as long as sampling is done at the access-side interface before encapsulation or after decapsulation.

SLA / probe-based monitoring

  • BFD over VXLAN: BFD sessions ride inside the VXLAN tunnel; loss of the UDP/4789 path triggers BFD down. Timers: typical 300 ms detection (3×100 ms intervals) for DC fabrics.
  • BFD over SRv6: BFD sessions use the IPv6 underlay directly or are encapsulated into the SRv6 SID path; sub-50 ms detection is achievable on both encaps with 10 ms / 3× intervals on modern silicon.
  • TWAMP/STAMP probes for delay and loss measurement work on both encaps with no changes to the probe source/sink config — the probe packets traverse the same underlay path and encap headers are transparent to the measurement.
  • For SRv6, add a locator-reachability probe (simple ICMPv6 to the node SID) separate from the service-SID probe — this distinguishes underlay failures from service-SID programming failures.
  • Update OAM probe target addresses: VXLAN probes target VTEP loopbacks; SRv6 probes should target the node SID (End SID) for underlay health and the service SID for data-plane health.

Dashboards during dual-stack

  • Create a split-panel layout: left panel shows VXLAN-side counters (VNI encap/decap, VTEP tunnel state, IPv4 underlay health); right panel shows SRv6-side counters (SID counters, locator reachability, IPv6 underlay health).
  • The gateway is the only node with genuinely new visibility requirements: a cross-encap translation counter (how many packets were re-encapsulated in each direction) is the primary new metric. This counter confirms that the gateway is actively bridging traffic and that neither encap side has gone silent unexpectedly.
  • Add a "traffic balance" panel during dual-stack: plot VXLAN byte counters and SRv6 byte counters as a ratio — this gives an at-a-glance view of migration progress per service.
  • Pin gateway translation-failure counters to the top of the migration dashboard — a non-zero translation failure is an immediate escalation trigger.

Alerting drift to watch

  • Scale counter thresholds: SRv6 SID table growth is shaped differently from VNI growth — adding a new service adds one or more SIDs per node rather than one VNI per leaf. Review and recalibrate SID table utilization alerts before the migration reaches scale.
  • Encap-mismatch alerts: alert on any BGP EVPN route carrying a VXLAN encap extended community that is imported on an SRv6-only leaf — this indicates a misconfigured leaf or a gateway failure to re-originate with the correct encap.
  • Gateway translation-failure alerts: add a dedicated alert for any non-zero gateway translation-failure counter; threshold should be zero — any failure here means tenant traffic is being dropped at the gateway boundary.
  • VNI-keyed panel health: alert when a VNI-keyed counter that was active drops to zero unexpectedly — this distinguishes a successful migration (VNI counter goes to zero because traffic moved to SRv6) from a failure (VNI counter goes to zero because the path broke).
  • Audit all SNMP traps and event triggers that reference VXLAN interface names or VNI OIDs — update them to equivalent SRv6 OIDs or YANG paths before cutover, not after.

21. Decision Matrix — Migrate Now / Later / Stay

Not every operator should migrate. Here are the signals.

The right migration decision depends on where you are today — hardware, scale, organizational readiness, and business drivers all matter. Use the signals below honestly. If you cannot point to at least two strong "migrate now" signals, the project risk likely outweighs the reward at this time. This guide's §FM-2 covers the motivations in more depth.

Migrate now if…

  • SP + DC unified-underlay is a stated goal. SRv6 is the only encap that spans both domains natively; staying on VXLAN forecloses that convergence.
  • SRv6-capable silicon is already deployed. If leaves and spines already run ASICs with uSID support, the marginal cost of migration is primarily operational, not capital.
  • Scale is approaching VXLAN's practical ceiling. VNI space (16 M) is rarely the constraint — per-leaf MAC table size, BUM replication fan-out, and VTEP peer count are. If you are at 60–70 % of any of these ceilings, SRv6's locator-based scale model is worth the migration cost now.
  • Multi-domain TE is pulling DC traffic. SRv6 policy can steer tenant flows across the WAN; VXLAN cannot participate in SR-TE without tunneling overhead and a gateway at every domain boundary.
  • Vendor EOL is approaching on VXLAN-only platforms. A planned hardware refresh is the lowest-friction migration window — new hardware comes up native SRv6 and the VXLAN footprint shrinks naturally.
  • You have SRv6 operational expertise in-house or under contract. Tooling, runbooks, and trained staff are ready.

Migrate later if…

  • Hardware refresh is 1–3 years out. Migrating twice (software now, hardware at refresh) doubles operational risk and change-freeze windows. Wait for the refresh and migrate once on new silicon.
  • Current scale and tooling work fine. If VNI counts, MAC tables, and BUM replication are all well below ceilings, there is no operational pressure to migrate.
  • No business pull from SP convergence or multi-domain TE. VXLAN is a proven, stable encap. Without a clear business driver, migration is cost and risk without payoff.
  • Organizational SRv6 skill gap is significant. Schedule training and lab time first; migrate once the team can operate the new plane without heroics. A 6–12 month readiness investment before migration is normal.
  • Active features under development are VXLAN-specific. Freezing feature work for a migration is expensive; finish the feature cycle first.

Stay on VXLAN if…

  • Single-site, modest scale, no SP integration story. VXLAN is purpose-built for this deployment model and will continue to receive vendor support indefinitely at this scale.
  • No SRv6 operational expertise and no plan to build it. Running a technology the team cannot troubleshoot is a reliability risk that no architecture benefit justifies.
  • Hardware is locked to a vendor without an SRv6 roadmap. If the silicon vendor has no uSID support in the next two platform generations, migration would require a full rip-and-replace — evaluate that as a separate capital project, not an encap migration.
  • Multi-vendor interop requirements are complex. SRv6 interop across vendors in the same fabric is possible but has more rough edges than VXLAN today. If you run a strict multi-vendor fabric, wait for the ecosystem to mature.
